Independent Research Questions Observatory

A public register of questions arising from observation, professional experience, interdisciplinary enquiry and emerging societal changes—whether or not they become funded projects or published papers. It enables readers to preview possible enquiries and connect them to appropriate evidence, geographical comparisons and existing research work.

Why keep an independent questions register?

Funding institutions help shape research agendas by setting strategic remits, eligibility and assessment criteria. They do not determine all knowledge production: UKRI offers applicant-led and targeted funding; Wellcome supports discovery research; institutional researchers and independent researchers can also formulate questions without a specific call.

As contexts change, a previously overlooked question may become relevant to a funder, research team, public body or different research population. The observable contribution here is the documented formulation and structured examination of a question, not a claim to be the first person ever to think of it.

Attribution and status: The original register reflects questions documented or linked to existing work by 11 November 2026; additional exploratory questions were added 12 November 2026. That date is the register date, not a claim that each question was first conceived on that day. Earlier provenance must be supported by independently dated records. A question is not evidence that its implied hypothesis is true.

From a question to potential research

1. Record the question and its origin

Assign a persistent identifier, clear wording, first supported date, observation category and links to available source records. Protect personal and third-party information.

2. Establish what is already known

Search high-quality prior work; record strongest contrary evidence, established methods, remaining limitations and the precise uncertainty. Distinguish gaps in data, theory, context and implementation.

3. Specify testability and relevant populations

Define outcomes, comparisons, alternative explanations, feasible data sources, ethics and falsification criteria. State what would count against the hypothesis.

4. Add international extensions without assuming equivalence

Begin with England when relevant; then Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland; Europe as a separate regional comparison; subsequently Africa, the Americas, Asia, Oceania and the Pacific with appropriate subregional divisions. Legal definitions and source comparability must be checked per jurisdiction.

5. Map possible research interest—not guaranteed funding

Identify potential alignment with published funder remits or public consultations without implying endorsement, eligibility or active applications. A question can remain independent of any funding strategy.

6. Attach optional project or paper initiative

Link to prior outputs or a scoped research concept only where justified. Avoid creating fictional manuscript progress or presenting idea capture as a completed study.

7. Maintain revision and collaboration records

Document question revisions, competing interpretations, evidence dates, collaboration enquiries and subsequent published outcomes with a visible correction history.

Using questions for collaboration

Researchers, institutions and funders can cite a stable question ID when proposing a comparative study, methodological critique, policy assessment, replication or interdisciplinary collaboration. A question can be developed in multiple contexts without assuming a single paper or owner has answered it comprehensively. Contact or collaboration pathways may be added later subject to privacy and moderation controls.
