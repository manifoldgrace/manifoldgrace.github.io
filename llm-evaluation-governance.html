LLM evaluation & corrective feedback

My practice of examining AI responses, identifying failures, supplying corrective feedback and checking whether the next response addresses the problem.

Grace Nettey · Updated 29 October 2026

Evaluation is work

I assess how conversational AI interprets context, handles evidence and represents people and their experiences. When a response misses the point, applies inconsistent expectations or obscures uncertainty, I identify the problem, explain the required correction and review the revised answer. This involves analytical effort, domain knowledge and sustained human oversight.

My technical QA background informs this approach: observe behaviour, compare it with the requirement, investigate the discrepancy, communicate findings and check the correction. My AI ethics study adds attention to fairness, accountability and human consequences.

Practice description: iterative qualitative evaluation of LLM outputs for contextual fidelity, evidential consistency and potential representational harm, with corrective feedback and review of subsequent responses.

Six governance capabilities

1. Human-in-the-loop evaluation

Review outputs, question unsupported conclusions and intervene when the response does not meet the task. Governance relevance: meaningful oversight and accountable use.

2. Qualitative LLM testing

Check contextual understanding, accuracy, relevance and consistency across a conversation. Governance relevance: evaluating fitness for the intended use.

3. Fairness and representational harm

Examine whether responses minimise experiences, misrepresent an argument or apply uneven evidential expectations. Governance relevance: identifying potential harms and examining whose knowledge is recognised.

4. Conversational failure analysis

Identify where context is lost, assumptions replace evidence or the system answers a different question. Governance relevance: documenting failure modes and requirements for improvement.

5. Remediation and retesting

Provide a specific correction and assess whether the revised answer resolves it. Governance relevance: following issues through to a checked outcome.

6. User-informed assurance

Bring lived experience, technical knowledge and institutional understanding to evaluation. Governance relevance: testing assumptions against the experiences of affected users.

Practice example: preserving the actual question

Context: a discussion on 29 October 2026 concerned AI governance recruitment, historical institutional exclusion and the expertise developed through encountering unfair systems.

Issue I identified: the assistant moved towards individual hiring fairness before adequately addressing my question about responsibility for historically unfair structures. I challenged this loss of emphasis and requested historical cross-referencing.

My intervention: I clarified the intended subject, requested supporting archival evidence and then examined whether the repeated act of correction itself constituted relevant AI evaluation work.

Observed outcome: the assistant revised its framing, supplied historical sources and acknowledged that the correction involved qualitative evaluation and human oversight. This records improvement within that conversation; it does not establish persistent improvement across other sessions or users.

Assessment criteria: preserve the user's intended question; distinguish structural history from individual attribution; support historical claims; recognise relevant experience; state the limits of conclusions.

Source: my retained conversation of 29 October 2026. This is a self-documented qualitative case, not a controlled benchmark or independent fairness audit.

A repeatable evaluation record

  1. Record context: task, date, intended use and available model/version information.
  2. Preserve the relevant output: retain the wording needed to understand the issue, with private information protected.
  3. Define the discrepancy: identify what was inaccurate, omitted, inconsistent or potentially harmful.
  4. Set an assessment criterion: explain what an acceptable response must do.
  5. Supply corrective feedback: specify the change and its reasoning.
  6. Retest: review the next output and note resolved and remaining issues.
  7. Track escalation: distinguish submitted feedback, acknowledgement, confirmed action and verified outcome.

This method makes individual observations more useful for issue review, governance reporting and regression-test design. Repeated examples can reveal patterns requiring further investigation; a single exchange does not establish their prevalence.

Training, evaluation and scope

In everyday language, repeated instruction and correction can be called training. The precise description of the work shown here is interactive LLM evaluation and corrective feedback. An improved reply within a conversation does not demonstrate a change to the underlying model parameters. These records do not establish whether particular feedback entered a provider's training pipeline.

The work supports qualitative assurance, human oversight and governance improvement. Statistical model validation, controlled performance benchmarks and training-pipeline ownership are separate activities requiring their own evidence.
