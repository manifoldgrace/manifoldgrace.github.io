Historical reconstruction: shared evidentiary standard

1. Before sustained Atlantic contact

Material culture, languages, settlement, land, ritual and household organisation. Chronology must be particular to each region.

2. Contact and regional transformation

Trace trade, conflict, alliances, migration, religious practice and institutional change; avoid assuming every custom remained fixed.

3. Colonial administration and later records

Compare court files, land claims, mission registers, civil registration and modern oral histories with earlier evidence.

Evidence rules: primary document or artefact; scholarly interpretation; attributed oral tradition; and unresolved or conflicting claim are distinct categories. A surname, contemporary ORCID profile or chiefly title does not itself prove kinship.

AI and the records future generations inherit

AI can help make hard-to-read archives searchable, but should not silently construct relationships. A useful system preserves the source image and transcription, labels uncertainty, and permits human correction.

  1. Capture: digitise letters, registers, court records, oral-history recordings, photographs and maps with consent and provenance.
  2. Transcribe: use handwriting recognition and language support, retaining the original alongside any AI-generated text.
  3. Resolve identities cautiously: compare names, aliases, places and dates; propose candidate links without auto-merging people.
  4. Map evidence: connect each relationship claim to exact document pages or oral testimonies, plus corroboration and contradictions.
  5. Preserve family control: restrict information about living people, sensitive lineage claims and culturally restricted knowledge; support corrections and takedowns.
  6. Export for future generations: use open formats, accessible backups and version histories; consider GEDCOM 7 for family-tree exchange and Records in Contexts for wider archival relationships.

Possible prototype: Evidence-linked ancestry graph

Person → claimed relationship → supporting record → repository URL/page → date → reviewer → verification status. Add alternative spellings, language/script, maternal and paternal links, opposing evidence, and a revision history. ORCID is a researcher identifier, not a genealogy registry.

Four-corridor archival retrieval and evidence guide

Added 8 November 2026. The following is an evidence-first research framework. References to archives, cultural practices and shared surnames are not themselves evidence of direct family relationships.

Ghana: Gbese, Ngleshie-Alata and Ga-Dangme

Scholarly interpretation. Historical work addresses mixed coastal and inland livelihoods, naming systems, ritual and secular authorities and changing settlement patterns. Interpretations differ across periods and Ga quarters. Individual family links remain unresolved.

  • M. J. Field — Social Organization of the Ga People
  • Irene Odotei — Pre-colonial Economic Activities of Ga
  • Abraham Akrong — Ga pre-monarchical leadership
  • PRAAD Ghana — national archives
  • British Library Endangered Archives Programme EAP541

Next test: identify dated primary land, court, mission or family-house records naming individual relatives, then evaluate each parent–child link.

Nigeria: Ondo Kingdom and compound histories

Scholarly interpretation. Ondo historiography identifies multiple levels of kindred and lineage organisation; maternal and paternal ties and the rights they confer require locality-specific evaluation. Oral tradition: origin stories from Ile-Ife, Oyo and Benin require separate attribution and chronological scrutiny.

  • Yoruba from Prehistory to the Present — kinship and society
  • P. C. Lloyd — Yoruba Land Law
  • Ondo women's governance and Lobun
  • University of Ibadan bibliography — named National Archives, Ibadan references
  • National Archives of Nigeria

Next test: obtain archival descriptions and original files for the 1933/1936 intelligence reports, examine their wording before assigning any claim about named compounds or Olokere relatives.

Cameroon: Bazou, Ndé and chiefly succession

Attributed local history. Bazou-specific studies describe traditional authority, quarters and succession narratives. Wider Bamileke ethnography supplies comparative context; findings from Bangwa or Bangoua must not automatically be applied to Bazou. Variant spellings Kemajou / Kemadjou are a search lead, not proof of relationship.

  • Kengne Nsidjine — Bazou-focused study
  • Jean Hurault — La structure sociale des Bamiléké
  • Bamileke kinship study — comparison only
  • MINAC — National Archives project
  • Cameroon official civil-registration portal

Next test: confirm the relevant Bazou quarter and request original chiefly, civil-status and administrative files. Archive access processes, email addresses, fees and reference codes must be reconfirmed with the current institution rather than assumed from secondary accounts.

Britain: civil, adoptive, Midlands and historical family lines

Documentary pathway. Modern birth, marriage, death, adoption and probate registers are distinct from medieval and early modern parish, manorial and noble family records. An adoptive relationship is genealogically meaningful in its own right; biological links require separate substantiation. Names such as Symondson, Simmonds, Deakin, Homer, Keeling, Hodgetts, Greswold, Lyttelton, Talbot, Paston and Beaufort are search targets, not established bridges across centuries.

  • General Register Office — certificates and registers
  • Adopted Children Register — statutory protections (not freely open to inspection)
  • Probate search service
  • The National Archives — PCC wills in PROB 11 (1384–1858)
  • The National Archives — Discovery search
  • Staffordshire heritage and archives
  • Worcestershire Archive & Archaeology Service
  • Warwickshire County Record Office
  • Norfolk Record Office

Next test: work backwards through individually dated records; search probate by person, parish and date, and distinguish original wills from registered copies. PROB 11 is not a universal wills collection.

Common claim-register schema

For each proposed fact: claim ID · jurisdiction and period · place · subject · original source URL and reference · page or image · extracted quotation or transcription · translation · record type · author/researcher · date accessed · evidence class (primary record / scholarly interpretation / attributed oral tradition / unresolved) · corroboration · contradictions · privacy permissions · status of human review.

ORCID researcher-profile searches should remain in a separate discovery register; use fielded family-name queries where supported and do not infer lineage from matches. No one should be automatically merged into a family tree based solely on names or geographical proximity.

British surname ORCID search register

ORCID indexes researchers, not historical families. Fielded queries use family-name:; researcher identity, institutional affiliations and any family relationship must be checked independently. The result count from a general search should not be reused as a filtered count.
