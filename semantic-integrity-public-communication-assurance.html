Semantic Integrity and Public Communication Assurance

A research programme examining when official language permits materially misleading interpretations; who is responsible for prevention and correction; and how AI and human review can make public information clearer without erasing legitimate uncertainty.

Conceptual research framework · 5 November 2026 · Source-grounded case studies distinguished from constructed examples and untested proposals

Core principle: clarity is a duty of publication, not only reading

A public communication should not pass assurance merely because its author can defend one intended reading. It should also be assessed for reasonably foreseeable, materially false impressions among the populations whose rights, duties, safety, finances or decisions the communication can affect. Readers can misunderstand clear text, but publishers also have a duty to anticipate plausible harmful interpretations and correct avoidable ambiguity.

Ambiguity is not identical to factual falsehood; materially misleading effect is not identical to proven deliberate deception. A rare fanciful reading should not automatically disqualify a sentence. The relevant test is whether a significant misunderstanding is plausible in context, consequential, reasonably foreseeable and remediable through proportionate changes. The original record and correction history must be retained.

AI responsibility: a drafting or review model may contribute to errors or omissions, but is not a substitute for a named human content owner, legal reviewer and accountable institution. A model's confident suggestion is a hypothesis for validation, not independent evidence that the public is misled. Government's own AI Playbook discusses hallucination, human oversight and organisational accountability.

Eight sourced case studies and institutional examples

Evidence labels matter: some entries are historical cases with independent criticism or inquiry evidence; others are an analysis of current text or official risk and prevention guidance. No constructed harm is represented as an observed incident.

1. Ministerial Code: public service, privilege and entitlement Primary-document close reading · interpretive risk, not evidenced harm

Documented basis: The June 2026 Ministerial Code foreword frames service in office as a privilege, contrasted with politicians treating service as an entitlement; clause 1.5 specifies ministerial office.

Potential mechanism: The noun phrase “public service” can refer to holding office, delivering services, or services received. A detached excerpt may blur whose entitlement is in question.

Consequential risk and evidence limit: A reader might wrongly infer that lawful service users should feel undeserving; that reading is not established as prevalent and is weaker in the original surrounding context.

Possible clearer wording / control: Holding ministerial office is a privilege carrying duties to the public. This does not diminish the legal entitlements of people eligible for public services.

Research test: Compare full-context and detached-excerpt comprehension; separately ask what the statement means for ministers and for service users.

Primary or scholarly source

2. Pandemic slogans: “Stay home” versus “Stay alert” Documented communication controversy · 2020

Documented basis: A 2020 King's College London analysis reported that only around 30% in an initial poll felt the new Stay Alert message clearly communicated what they should do.

Potential mechanism: Replacing an executable instruction with an open-ended behavioural metaphor makes the action criterion uncertain.

Consequential risk and evidence limit: Different readers can choose different behaviours while believing they complied; downstream health impact cannot be quantified from a slogan alone.

Possible clearer wording / control: State the actual required or recommended actions, the date and the jurisdiction; distinguish legal rules from advice.

Research test: Run task-based comprehension and behavioural-choice tests across reading levels and languages.

Primary or scholarly source

3. Four nations: cross-jurisdictional restrictions Parliamentary inquiry finding · 2021

Documented basis: A joint Commons committee report describes divergent messages across UK nations during the May 2020 easing, and evidence reporting confusing or apparently contradictory statements.

Potential mechanism: A UK-wide political or media frame can obscure which jurisdiction's instructions apply.

Consequential risk and evidence limit: Readers may apply rules from another nation; assessment must distinguish genuine contradiction from different valid local policies.

Possible clearer wording / control: For England only, from [date], [rule]. Different requirements may apply in Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland; consult the relevant administration.

Research test: Use mixed-location comprehension tasks and verify users identify jurisdiction, effective date and legal status.

Primary or scholarly source

4. Guidance presented as law House of Lords scrutiny · 2021

Documented basis: The House of Lords Constitution Committee identified examples where public-health advice, guidance and regulations were conflated or guidance was enforced as law.

Potential mechanism: Imperatives such as must, should, required and advised are applied without naming the legal instrument.

Consequential risk and evidence limit: People may comply under a mistaken view of legal penalties or challenge actual duties incorrectly.

Possible clearer wording / control: Specify whether a requirement is law or nonbinding guidance, the legal source, exceptions, enforcement body and commencement date.

Research test: Compare legal-status classification accuracy for original versus redrafted notices.

Primary or scholarly source

5. A government AI chatbot giving false service advice Official government risk example · not a proven UK incident

Documented basis: The 2025 UK AI Playbook explicitly warns that an LLM-powered public chatbot could hallucinate and mislead a person about a government service, policy or legal point; it says organisational responsibility remains.

Potential mechanism: Fluent generation can insert invented eligibility conditions, omit exceptions or turn a probabilistic inference into asserted fact.

Consequential risk and evidence limit: Users may miss entitlements, deadlines, appeals or needed support, especially if they reasonably trust an official interface.

Possible clearer wording / control: Use retrieval from current authoritative rules, disclose uncertainty, provide official source and human correction paths; do not present unverified advice as a determination.

Research test: Test with known eligibility edge cases, conflicting policy versions, out-of-scope questions and appeals.

Primary or scholarly source

6. Home Office word choice: “abroad” and “overseas” Official style guidance · ambiguity acknowledged

Documented basis: The Home Office content guide specifically notes that abroad and overseas can confuse and suggests clearer contextual alternatives.

Potential mechanism: A relative term may fail to specify the relevant legal geographic boundary, place of application or residence.

Consequential risk and evidence limit: A person can select the wrong application route or misunderstand whether their circumstances qualify.

Possible clearer wording / control: Apply from outside the UK; or work in another country, as appropriate to the rule.

Research test: Give representative users realistic application scenarios and compare route-selection errors.

Primary or scholarly source

7. Government AI accountability and validation Official framework · governance requirement, not specific harm event

Documented basis: The AI Playbook calls for human control at relevant stages, testing of outputs and traceable accountability across the lifecycle.

Potential mechanism: AI-origin text may be published without verifying interpretation, temporal accuracy, legal provenance or the user impact of wrong advice.

Consequential risk and evidence limit: The institutional publisher can inadvertently amplify automated inaccuracies at scale.

Possible clearer wording / control: Require named content owner, source citations, risk-scoped user testing, audit trace and documented corrections before publication.

Research test: Measure AI and human review detection rates against independent expert labels.

Primary or scholarly source

8. ONS stakeholder check for misleading or missing material Published institutional quality-control method · preventative example

Documented basis: The ONS content guide asks stakeholders to identify content that is incorrect, misleading or missing, while peer reviewers assess clarity and structure.

Potential mechanism: A factual sentence can mislead through omitted qualification, inconsistent headings, missing context or implied scope.

Consequential risk and evidence limit: Literal correctness may pass a narrow fact check while the whole user journey conveys a false operational impression.

Possible clearer wording / control: Add a separate material-misinterpretation check alongside factual verification and accessibility testing.

Research test: Use blinded reviewers to identify omissions, then track error reduction in the published user journey.

Primary or scholarly source

Proposed publication assurance pipeline

  1. Source control: store document, author, approver, jurisdiction, purpose, audience, policy source, effective dates and prior wording.
  2. Claim extraction: identify operative statements about rights, eligibility, duties, safety, sanctions, deadlines, costs or access.
  3. Lexical and semantic audit: use dictionaries, legal definitions and domain terminology to test alternate meanings; inspect syntax, reference, scope, modality and omissions.
  4. Contextual adversarial reading: test the whole page, snippet, notification, letter and translation; distinguish plausible from fanciful interpretations.
  5. Risk triage: identify material impact, affected users, probability, reversibility and any severe credible downside; green = sufficiently supported, amber = unresolved, red = foreseeable serious harm requiring correction.
  6. Independent human validation: content designers, source owners, legal and accessibility specialists assess AI-generated flags, retaining dissent and uncertainty.
  7. Audience comprehension: test with realistic tasks and representative users, including those with accessibility needs and low prior knowledge.
  8. Constrained redrafting: use precise descriptive wording without falsely simplifying legal complexity or changing a policy's meaning.
  9. Publication gate: publish when material issues are resolved or appropriately disclosed; retain named accountability and evidence of sign-off.
  10. Post-publication correction: preserve previous versions, report discovered harm, correct promptly, monitor recurrence and provide redress where appropriate.

Automation boundary: an AI flag is not a legal judgement, and automated replacement should not overwrite the authoritative original without human approval. Information security and confidential draft handling are part of the assurance design.

Responsibility and remedy

Reader: may need to seek clarification where text is clearly incomplete; this does not eliminate publisher obligations. Author/editor: should provide the degree of precision reasonably required for the context and audience. Publisher and approving organisation: own the publication process, its controls and timely corrections. AI developer/deployer: may contribute design, training, testing or deployment failures; legal responsibility depends on the applicable facts and law, while public organisations remain responsible for their communications.

Do not confuse accidental ambiguity, objectively misleading effect, negligent review and intentional deception. Only the latter requires evidence of intent; all can motivate proportionate prevention or correction. A reader's unexpected interpretation is a signal to investigate—not automatic proof of publisher misconduct.
