Administrative Decisions, Evidence Access and Review: An Anonymised Chronology

A longitudinal working record of educational activity and concurrent administrative processes from 2024 to 2026. This page documents a research method and preliminary chronology; it is not a judicial finding, accredited qualification or complete court evidence bundle.

Status: Working chronology — event classifications and primary-record reconciliation ongoing. Updated: November 2026.

Purpose and limitations

This chronology studies how educational and administrative decisions, evidence availability, procedural deadlines and independent review mechanisms can interact. It aims to distinguish what a decision actually determined from what it closed without substantive examination.

The public record uses generalised periods and non-identifying descriptions. Identifying details and any sensitive underlying evidence are excluded entirely. Public evidence keys are generic research identifiers, not links to personal records. This document is not a claim that any unidentified party acted unlawfully.

Decision integrity test

For each potential breach, distinguish: (a) recorded event, (b) applicable rule or obligation, (c) what the rule required in the circumstances, (d) disputed departure, (e) available counter-evidence, and (f) independent outcome, if any. Descriptions of possible misconduct do not imply an offence has occurred.

Research proposition: An issue-level disposition register may reduce the risk that a procedural closure is subsequently treated as a substantive determination. This proposition is untested here.

Five decisive pivot points

Second upload: inspect what students were told about replacing an existing submission and penalty consequences. Unreadable work: examine the emailed copy and technical record before treating work as absent. Reassessment: identify the eligible attempt, actual offer and notified date. Appeal: keep administrative error, deadline admissibility and merits separate. Records access: examine whether late disclosure undermined the ability to meet another deadline.

Interpretive limit: repeated time limits may have produced cumulative disadvantage; deliberate misuse or intentional obstruction is a hypothesis requiring separate proof.

Alternative submission routes: procedural duty, not merely a platform setting

The existence of a standard electronic submission channel does not, on its own, resolve whether an institution was required to consider an alternative means of receiving and assessing work in the particular circumstances. A copy sent through another channel should be assessed alongside contemporaneous communication, technical records, any request for a procedural variation, and the relevant rules. The question is not whether every emailed document automatically counts as a valid assessment submission; it is whether the institution considered and explained any necessary and reasonable adjustment to its process rather than treating the standard channel as an absolute bar.

Potential decision pivot: verify that the emailed attachment was received and readable, preserve its timestamp and contents, identify a lawful assessment route or comparable opportunity, and record a reasoned acceptance or refusal. If an alternative was considered and rejected, examine the documented justification and whether another effective arrangement was offered.

Independent legal reference: Equality Act 2010, section 20 (duty to make adjustments); section 91 (higher education students). Listing a legal source does not establish that any duty was breached or identify the private circumstances of a particular case.

Temporal and legal caution: regulatory duties, institutional policy, statutory rights and non-binding good practice are not equivalent. The rules in force when each event occurred must be established. A time limit, by itself, is not evidence of wrongdoing: the issue is whether the applicable rule was correctly applied and whether other lawful routes were properly considered.

Issue-level disposition method

Every issue should receive its own evidence identifier and a status selected from: not yet verified; received but not determined; procedurally closed; determined on merits; under review; or corrected. Any classification must identify the primary document and the exact scope of the decision. Different legal or administrative questions may arise from common facts, but a changed description alone does not necessarily create a new complaint.

Questions for investigation

  2. How do delayed records and communications interact with procedural deadlines?
  3. Can issue-level disposition records reduce erroneous duplication classifications?
  4. Which safeguards permit correction without recreating an entire concluded proceeding?
