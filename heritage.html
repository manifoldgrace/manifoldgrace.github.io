My grandparents — lives and contributions

I want both of my grandfathers and both of my grandmothers to be remembered for their own lives. This record values paid work, skill, care, faith, hospitality, community service and the knowledge passed between generations.

Paternal grandfather · 21 December 1934 – 15 May 2023

Christopher Akai “C.A.” Nettey

A teacher, mental-health nurse, ward manager and nursing examiner whose care extended from professional practice to family and community life.

  • Education and nursing: taught in Accra, trained in Ghana and Britain, and progressed to ward management.
  • Community and hospitality: helped relatives and friends settle in Britain, supported GaDangme organisations and shared his home generously.
  • Faith and family history: donated a christening font in his mother’s memory and preserved extensive genealogical research.
Career, service and achievements catalogue

Teaching and professional development

  • Completed Standard Seven education in 1950 and continued at Odogono Secondary School / West Africa College, as named in his biography.
  • Taught at Methodist Primary School, Adabraka, later known as Master Bruce Memorial School.
  • Undertook evening teaching and Adult Education Unit work at Adentro School, as recorded in the booklet.
  • Entered psychiatric nursing at the hospital in Asylum-Down, Accra, qualifying there before further UK training.
  • Trained and qualified in mental-health nursing at Long Grove Hospital, Epsom, Surrey.
  • Began further nursing training at St James’ Hospital, Balham, in 1964.
  • Progressed through staff nurse and charge nurse roles to ward manager.
  • Appointed as a practical-nursing examiner in 1982, according to the funeral biography.
  • Retired as a ward manager from Long Grove Hospital in 1992.

Recognition, faith and public service

  • Received an invitation to a Buckingham Palace garden party in June 1975, as recorded in his biography.
  • Supported charitable functions and societies in Ghana and Britain.
  • Held senior and stewardship roles in the GaDangme Nikasemo Society and lodges. The GaDangme tribute specifically recalls his management of the organisation’s treasury.
  • Donated a christening font to All Saints’ Anglican Church, Adabraka, in memory of Grace Adjuah Sackey-Jacobson.

Care, skills and cultural memory

  • Welcomed and advised newcomers from Ghana, with accommodation, meals and practical support described in the biography and friends’ tribute.
  • Collected family histories and genealogical information from older relatives, preserving relationships and branches for later generations.
  • Encouraged children and grandchildren through puzzles, games, conversation and learning; the tributes recall chess, draughts and wari.
  • Remembered for humour, listening, encouragement, hymns and devotional messages, and efforts to keep the family connected.

Sources: funeral biography beginning on printed page 10 and its continuation; children’s, grandchildren’s, GaDangme and friends’ tributes; Grace’s account of his genealogy work. Private descendant names are omitted. Family-source note.

Gbese and Jamestown — kinship as diplomacy

The Gbese and Ngleshie-Alata connections recorded in my great-grandparents’ family biography sit within a wider history of coastal settlement, marriage and political negotiation. Reindorf’s History of the Gold Coast and Asante (1895) offers three memorable examples:

  • Migration and belonging: his account describes English, Dutch and Danish Alata communities forming quarters and participating in local civic life.
  • Marriage and peace: he recounts marriages linking Okaidsha and Kpakpo Amoaforo’s family, which he credits with peace between James Town, Abora and Gbese.
  • Leadership across quarters: he identifies James Town’s King Kodsho Ababio as belonging to Gbese, and describes kinship among the considerations behind a negotiated settlement.

Before the nineteenth-century timeline — Nii Doku and the 1730s tradition

The historical account supplied to this enquiry identifies Nii Doku as an earlier foundational figure associated with Nii Doku We and Gbese Sonmenaa (also written Somnenaa in the supplied account). It places this tradition in the first half of the eighteenth century, before the existing timeline beginning with Asafoatse Nii Tetteh Nettey.

1730–1733 — the supplied ancestral account

According to the supplied account, Nii Doku defended the Ga community and forged iron tools and weapons for the Gbese contingent during what it calls the fourth Ga–Akwamu War, dated 1730–1733. It attributes the establishment of a senior war-captain position to this service and describes his descendants as connected to the Nettey/Nertey family of Sonmenaa.

The account uses the terms Asafoatse-ngua and Asafoatse-nukpa. Their precise usage, the claimed appointment, the dates and the intervening generations require the underlying traditional account and supporting records. These details are retained as an attributed research lead, not as a completed direct pedigree.

Earlier Ga history — tenth to fifteenth centuries and beyond

Ayawaso and early leadership — late medieval and early modern research

Ayawaso is an important place to investigate the earlier Ga state and its inland settlements. The supplied account places a flourishing kingdom in the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries; the exact dating needs archaeological and historical support. University of Ghana archaeological research discusses a Portuguese mission to the kingdom of Accra in 1517 and considers its implications for Ayawaso’s earlier existence.

Ayi Kushie, also encountered as Ayi Kushi, appears in accounts of early Ga spiritual and political leadership. His chronology, the variations between traditions and the relationship of particular stools to that history are research questions. He is retained here as a historical lead, rather than an established root ancestor of my personal pedigree.

Community formation, inland trade and changing authority
1 — Kpeshi interaction and religious continuity

The supplied material highlights indigenous Kpeshi communities, lagoon-centred religious custodianship, intermarriage and cultural exchange as themes in Ga community formation. A family-history essay citing M. J. Field places some settlement among Kpeshi communities near the end of the sixteenth century, illustrating why a uniform tenth-to-thirteenth-century chronology should not be assumed.

The roles of coastal shrines, land custodians, kinship and iron-working deserve separate investigation. The supplied identification of Nyonmo as a lagoon deity and the claim that migration introduced metallurgy require specific supporting sources; they are retained as questions rather than established conclusions. Intermarriage also does not demonstrate that all encounters were peaceful.

Family-history essay · cited traditions and differing migration chronologies
2 — Ayawaso: inland capital, Ayi Kushi and trade

Ghana’s National Commission on Culture describes Ayawaso’s rise in the fifteenth century and locates it inland near present-day Pokuase. It attributes its founding to Ayi Kushi in tradition, and describes leadership combining political and religious authority.

Its position connected inland producers with coastal exchange, including trade in gold, ivory and agricultural goods. The Commission places its destruction by Akwamu forces approximately between 1677 and 1680 and relates this to subsequent coastal settlement. These are collective historical accounts; they do not themselves establish a personal ancestral link.

National Commission on Culture · Ayawaso and the Ga Dangme world
3 — Spiritual, political and military institutions

The supplied account proposes a transition from priestly authority to military and chiefly lineages under regional pressures, with Akan influence on institutions and terminology. Research themes include Wulomei, Mantsemei, Asafoatse and Jaase, whose functions and histories should be examined in their particular communities.

The claim that secular kings did not exist throughout the tenth to fifteenth centuries, or that all these offices originated at one moment, is not established here. The Commission’s account describes combined religious and political authority. Institutional borrowing and change need not mean that one system simply replaced another.

IDENTITY · PLACE · MIGRATION · INDIVIDUAL EXPERIENCE

Black Britishness and connected global histories

My perspective: Black Britishness deserves to be understood in its own historical, social and personal context, while retaining its connections to African heritage and wider Black global histories. Connection does not require identical identities or experiences.

People born or raised in Britain or the United States may understand themselves differently from people born and raised in African countries. Shared ancestry, family connections and migration histories can remain meaningful without making those identities interchangeable. Individuals also differ in how they describe their own belonging.

The circumstances faced in Britain can differ from those in Ghana. Within Britain, experiences in London, Wales and Scotland can differ; Ireland also needs its own geographical, national and local context, including the distinction between Northern Ireland and the Republic of Ireland. Similarities and mutual understanding can coexist with substantial differences.

Recognising connection without collapsing difference

Race intersects with nationality, locality, generation, class, gender, religion, disability, education, family history and migration experience. The combination can create circumstances distinctive to an individual or community. Relatability does not mean every situation is equivalent.

Treating all Black experience as one universal category can obscure the institutions, conditions and individual histories that explain a particular situation. Careful analysis asks where, when, under which circumstances and for whom an experience occurs.

My aim is to preserve both the connections and the distinctions: shared histories and solidarity alongside the truthfulness of individual experience. Differences should be examined rather than overlooked, and a person’s own account of identity should remain central.

Read the supplied comparison — Ghana and England

To prove noble or royal descent, Ghana relies on oral history and custom validated by regional councils, while England relies on strict documentary evidence managed by central statutory authorities. [1, 2, 3, 4]

The entire concept of "nobility" operates differently in each country. In England, it is tied to titles and coats of arms granted to individuals. In Ghana, it is tied to traditional leadership (chieftaincy) and membership in a specific royal "gate" or family lineage. [1, 2]

How to Prove Descent in Ghana

In Ghana, proving noble descent is deeply tied to the Chieftaincy Act of 2008. If you claim to be a royal or eligible for a "stool" (Southern Ghana) or "skin" (Northern Ghana), you must prove you hail from the appropriate royal family or "gate". [1, 2, 3]

  1. Verify the Royal Gate: You must prove your lineage traces back to a recognized ruling house or gate. For example, the Waala monarchy formally recognizes four distinct royal gates. [1, 2]
  2. Kingmaker and Queen Mother Testimony: Customary law places massive weight on living repositories of history. In Akan traditions, the Queen Mother holds the ultimate authority on who belongs to the royal lineage and nominates heirs. [1, 2]
  3. Traditional Council Records: You must consult the minutes, dispute resolutions, and local historical records kept by the specific Traditional Council. [1]
  4. The National Register: The final administrative proof of verified status is having the lineage formalised and published in the government Gazette by the National House of Chiefs. [1, 2]

How to Prove Descent in England

In England, family legends or DNA tests carry no weight under heraldic law. Proving a right to an existing coat of arms or a peerage title is an exact legal and bureaucratic process handled in London. [1, 2, 3]

  1. Trace the Legitimate Male Line: To inherit a coat of arms or a title, you must generally prove an unbroken, legitimate paternal chain back to the original person granted the arms. Illegitimate lines are excluded from inheriting arms. [1, 2]
  2. Consult Official Registers: You must work with an Officer of Arms at the College of Arms to search their official historic registers, which date back centuries. [1, 2]
  3. Submit a Formal Pedigree: You must build a tree where every single birth, marriage, and death certificate is provided as evidence. Two independent heralds must meticulously examine and verify every link. [1]
  4. Statutory Declaration: For peerage titles, a formal petition is submitted to the House of Lords and a Statutory Declaration is sent to the Lord Chancellor and Secretary of State for Justice. [1]

Questions to use when checking the comparison
  • Does a source concern ancestry, membership of a royal family, eligibility for a stool or skin, installation as a chief, inheritance of arms, or succession to a specific peerage? These are different questions.
  • Which community’s customary succession rules apply? Ghana’s Constitution refers to the appropriate customary law and usage and to registration of people as chiefs; it does not make every descendant a registered chief.
  • Does an English source describe a right to arms or succession to a particular title? Those requirements should not be treated as a universal test of all royal ancestry.
  • What record supports each parent–child link, and what does the relevant council, herald or authority actually certify?
