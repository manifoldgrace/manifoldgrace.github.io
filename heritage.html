Heritage

My heritage is being reconstructed through two principal family corridors: a paternal Ga-Dangme line rooted in Accra, Ghana, and a maternal English line that includes both biological ancestry and legally established adoptive-family history.

DOCUMENTED IMMEDIATE LINE / EARLIER BRIDGE UNDER RESEARCH

01 — Ghanaian corridor

My paternal family is Ga-Dangme from Accra. The immediate family chain preserved in family records runs from me through my father to my paternal grandfather Christopher Akai Nettey (died 15 May 2023, aged 88) and to my great-grandfather Herbert Mills Nettey. My grandfather undertook substantial genealogical research into elders, descendants, wives, children and family branches; recovering and digitising those papers is now one of the highest-priority tasks in the archive.

The broader historical association with Swalaba, Sonmenaa and Gbese is increasingly well-supported. A 2024 Ghana News Agency report identifies the Asafoatse C.B. Nettey Family as a family of the Swalaba Quarter of Sonmenaa in Gbese and records a sequence of Gbese Asafoatse office-holders. This establishes an important historical and geographical family environment; it does not, by itself, fill every parent-to-child generation between my documented line and those earlier office-holders.

A 1936 West African Court of Appeal case records Akai Kofi Nettey as the eldest surviving son of Nii Nettey Quarshie in litigation concerning property at Horse Road, Ussher Town. This is a genuine parent-child relationship within the historical Nettey record, but Herbert Mills Nettey's exact placement in that branch remains more to explore.

The British Library's Endangered Archives Programme separately catalogues EAP1161/1/3/3/121, Christopher Brandford Nettey of Horse Road, and EAP1161/1/3/3/122, E. Q. Nettey of Accra, both dated 20 September 1947. These building-permit files provide fixed municipal and property anchors for the continuing research; consecutive catalogue placement or shared surnames are not treated as proof of biological relationship without the underlying documents.

Evidence rules for the Ghanaian line

  • Documented family line: Grace → father → Christopher Akai Nettey → Herbert Mills Nettey.
  • Strongly supported historical environment: Ga-Dangme Accra; Swalaba / Sonmenaa / Gbese; the public history of the Asafoatse C.B. Nettey family.
  • More to explore: Herbert Mills Nettey's parents, siblings, exact family gate or house, and the precise generation-by-generation bridge into the earlier Asafoatse Nettey lineage.
  • Identity safeguards: my grandfather Christopher Akai Nettey is kept distinct from Asafoatse Christopher Akai Nettey IV (d. 2012), and Herbert Mills Nettey is kept distinct from the contemporary family spokesman Herbert Akai Nettey unless primary evidence establishes a relationship.
  • Method: absence from an online index is treated as unresolved rather than as disproof; a relationship is rejected only where evidence actually contradicts it.

Parallel record under investigation

The 1959 High Court (Land Division) judgment Norquaye-Tetteh v. Malm & Anor. [1959] GLR 368 documents the Malm land transaction and suits 129/1958 and 130/1958. It is being used as a separate Nettey research branch and archival lead; it is not presently represented as proven direct ancestry.

Current archival frontier

The online phase is now deliberately paused at the point where primary physical records are required. The project will reopen when one or more of the following becomes available:

  1. My grandfather's original genealogy papers — to compare his lists of wives, children, elders and branches against the candidate historical lines.
  2. PRAAD / Judicial Service trial records for suits 129/1958 and 130/1958 — to inspect testimony, pleadings and exhibits that are not reproduced in the published judgment.
  3. Lands Commission deed material connected with the 3 December 1958 registration of the Malm deed — to inspect the original instrument, endorsements and witness block.

Current assessment: the broader family and geographic association is well-supported; the remaining task is to prove the exact parent-to-child and gate-to-gate bridges without filling gaps by assumption.

DOCUMENTED / SUPPORTED

02 — English corridor

My maternal heritage includes English biological ancestry alongside a significant legal-adoptive corridor through the Symondson family. Biological descent, legal adoption, collateral relationship and surname change are kept as separate genealogical bridges rather than being collapsed into one claim.

The published genealogy shows a pronounced musical thread within the wider Symondson / Simmonds family record. Henry Symondson (1832–1911) is recorded as a musician, band sergeant and staff sergeant; his son Henry John Symondson (1860–1926) as a musician with the Scots Guards; Alfred Stuart Symondson (1865–1930) as a professional musician, bandsman and trumpeter with the 11th Hussars before later Scots Guards service; and Sydney Frederick Symondson (1862–1927) as a musician-conductor and musical director. Frederick Thomas Symondson (c.1835–1882) is also recorded as a pianoforte maker, musician and professor of music. These people are presented as documented figures within the published family record rather than automatically as proven direct or collateral relatives of mine.

The musical pattern reaches further back through the connected Danby line. John Danby (1757–1798) is documented as a singer, organist, composer and professor of music, was elected to the Royal Society of Musicians, served as organist of the Spanish Embassy chapel and published prize-winning glees and sacred music. These records are included as historical features of the wider family network, not as evidence that musical ability itself is inherited.

Current likelihood assessment: the named musical occupations above are high-confidence within the present published source set because they are tied to census, parish, military, occupational and biographical records. What remains to be established precisely is how my grandmother's adoptive Symondson family connects genealogically to this documented branch. That bridge is kept separate from the already documented Symondson → Simmonds naming sequence and the later Simmonds–Deakin marriage.

DOCUMENTED BRIDGE / DEEPER PEDIGREE UNDER REVIEW

03 — Simmonds–Deakin bridge & deeper pedigree

The published genealogy documents the branch-level bridge directly: Alfred Stuart Symondson used the Simmonds form of the surname, and his son Alfred Henry Percy Simmonds married Margaret Patience Deakin in 1921. The Simmonds–Deakin marriage is therefore no longer treated here as the uncertain part of the reconstruction.

Margaret also extends the musical history of this branch. The 1911 census records her as a music teacher; the family genealogy states that she played piano, organ and violin and later served for decades as organist at St Mary's Church in Keatley. Percy is described in family testimony as musically adept when young, including a recollection of piano performance with an orchestra; that particular detail is retained as family memory rather than treated as independently established fact.

Published genealogy for the Deakin branch also traces a deeper collateral pedigree through the Beaufort family to John of Gaunt and Edward III. This remains a collateral adoptive-family connection, not a claim that my grandmother was biologically descended from those lines.

Current likelihood assessment: the Symondson / Simmonds → Deakin connection is documented within the present source set. The deeper Deakin-to-Beaufort-to-Plantagenet pedigree remains secondary-source supported until its relevant generations are independently checked against primary records. The important method is to preserve each bridge separately rather than treating legal adoption, collateral kinship, surname variation and biological descent as interchangeable.

UNDER RESEARCH

04 — Wider historical connections

The English corridor also intersects with histories of law, public service, military service, music and civic life. Proposed connections involving families such as Dolben, Digby and Noel remain under investigation and are not treated as established lineage here.

Current likelihood assessment: these wider connections remain plausible working hypotheses where names, marriages, occupations or published trees point in the same direction, but the evidence is not yet strong enough to label them probable. Rather than discarding them, the archive preserves them as research leads until supporting or contradictory records can be weighed.
