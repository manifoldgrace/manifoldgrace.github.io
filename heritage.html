Heritage

My heritage is being reconstructed through two principal family corridors: a paternal Ga-Dangme line rooted in Accra, Ghana, and a maternal English line that includes both biological ancestry and legally established adoptive-family history.

HIGHLY PROBABLE / UNDER RESEARCH

01 — Ghanaian corridor

My paternal family is rooted in coastal Accra, with family associations across Jamestown, Gbese, Ngleshie-Alata, Swalaba and Sonmenaa. Recent generations and family geography are supported through family records and oral history; the exact bridge into earlier generations remains under reconstruction.

My grandfather carried out substantial genealogical work on elders, descendants, family branches and the wider Asafoatse Nettey family tradition. That body of research is an important starting point for the present archive.

Current likelihood assessment: the broad Ga-Dangme / Accra family corridor is highly probable on the present family evidence, while the exact generational bridge into earlier named ancestors remains under research. Public GaDangme discussion archives also show Nettey participation in Ga civic and community life, but those records are treated as contextual support rather than proof that every similarly named person belongs to the same direct line.

DOCUMENTED / SUPPORTED

02 — English corridor

My maternal heritage includes English biological ancestry alongside a significant legal-adoptive corridor through the Symondson family. Biological descent, legal adoption, collateral relationship and surname change are kept as separate genealogical bridges rather than being collapsed into one claim.

The published family record shows a pronounced musical thread across the wider Symondson / Simmonds network. Henry Symondson (1832–1911) is recorded as a musician, band sergeant and staff sergeant; his son Henry John Symondson (1860–1926) as a musician with the Scots Guards; Alfred Stuart Symondson (1865–1930) as a professional musician, bandsman and trumpeter with the 11th Hussars before later Scots Guards service; and Sydney Frederick Symondson (1862–1927) as a musician-conductor and musical director. Frederick Thomas Symondson (c.1835–1882) is also recorded as a pianoforte maker, musician and professor of music.

The musical pattern reaches further back through the connected Danby line. John Danby (1757–1798) is documented as a singer, organist, composer and professor of music, was elected to the Royal Society of Musicians, served as organist of the Spanish Embassy chapel and published prize-winning glees and sacred music. These records are included as historical features of the wider family network, not as evidence that musical ability itself is inherited.

Current likelihood assessment: the named musical occupations above are high-confidence within the present published source set because they are tied to census, parish, military, occupational and biographical records. The relevant family bridges are kept distinct, but the documented Symondson → Simmonds sequence itself is not treated as an uncertain naming hypothesis.

DOCUMENTED BRIDGE / DEEPER PEDIGREE UNDER REVIEW

03 — Simmonds–Deakin bridge & deeper pedigree

The published genealogy documents the branch-level bridge directly: Alfred Stuart Symondson used the Simmonds form of the surname, and his son Alfred Henry Percy Simmonds married Margaret Patience Deakin in 1921. The Simmonds–Deakin marriage is therefore no longer treated here as the uncertain part of the reconstruction.

Margaret also extends the musical history of this branch. The 1911 census records her as a music teacher; the family genealogy states that she played piano, organ and violin and later served for decades as organist at St Mary's Church in Keatley. Percy is described in family testimony as musically adept when young, including a recollection of piano performance with an orchestra; that particular detail is retained as family memory rather than treated as independently established fact.

Published genealogy for the Deakin branch also traces a deeper collateral pedigree through the Beaufort family to John of Gaunt and Edward III. This remains a collateral adoptive-family connection, not a claim that my grandmother was biologically descended from those lines.

Current likelihood assessment: the Symondson / Simmonds → Deakin connection is documented within the present source set. The deeper Deakin-to-Beaufort-to-Plantagenet pedigree remains secondary-source supported until its relevant generations are independently checked against primary records. The important method is to preserve each bridge separately rather than treating legal adoption, collateral kinship, surname variation and biological descent as interchangeable.

UNDER RESEARCH

04 — Wider historical connections

The English corridor also intersects with histories of law, public service, military service, music and civic life. Proposed connections involving families such as Dolben, Digby and Noel remain under investigation and are not treated as established lineage here.

Current likelihood assessment: these wider connections remain plausible working hypotheses where names, marriages, occupations or published trees point in the same direction, but the evidence is not yet strong enough to label them probable. Rather than discarding them, the archive preserves them as research leads until supporting or contradictory records can be weighed.
