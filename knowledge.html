IDEATION · CRITICAL THOUGHT · OBJECTIVITY

Thinking

Knowledge gives us material to work with. Thinking examines that material, connects it, questions it and develops possibilities. Neither replaces the other: informed thinking remains open to evidence, experience and revision.

Careful thinkers attend to what they know, how they know it, what they assume and what could change their minds. Objectivity is a practice of making reasoning open to scrutiny—not a claim to have no perspective.

Purpose, questions & definitions

Clarify the problem, the decision and the meaning of key terms. Notice whether the question itself contains an assumption, narrows the options or leaves someone out.

Evidence & provenance

Distinguish observations, testimony, data, interpretation and speculation. Ask who produced or introduced a source, when, by which method and with what limitations. Preserve the contribution of people who locate and curate evidence.

Experience & context

Listen carefully to firsthand accounts and reconstruct the chain of events before judging it. Consider history, culture, institutions and circumstances; separate an observed event from a proposed mechanism or wider explanation.

Assumptions & mental models

Surface what is being taken for granted. Examine inherited categories and expectations. Ask whether a useful model has been mistaken for the whole reality.

Bias & intellectual humility

Notice confirmation bias, anchoring, availability, group pressure and motivated reasoning. Seek evidence that could challenge a preferred account and apply comparable standards to competing claims.

Logic & causal reasoning

Check whether conclusions follow from premises. Consider alternative causes, confounding factors, selection effects, timing and feedback loops. A correlation can prompt investigation without settling causation.

Perspectives & fair interpretation

Consider affected people, opposing arguments and absent voices. State an argument in a form its supporter would recognise before evaluating it. Openness does not require treating every claim as equally supported.

Ideation & imagination

Generate several possibilities before choosing one. Use analogy, sketches, mind maps, questions, cross-domain connections and “what if” scenarios. Keep idea generation spacious, then assess the ideas against evidence and constraints.

Objectivity & uncertainty

Make methods and reasoning explicit, distinguish fact from value judgement and record uncertainty. Ask what is missing, how confident a conclusion should be and what evidence would justify updating it.

Systems, ethics & consequences

Trace relationships, incentives, power and unintended effects. Consider who benefits, who carries costs, which values are at stake and how a proposal may work over different timescales.

Reflection & testing

Keep a reasoning journal, compare predictions with outcomes and invite constructive challenge. Use small experiments where appropriate. Record why a view changed so learning remains visible.

Conditions that support thought

Make room for focused attention, rest, walks, quiet, conversation and accessible tools. Capture ideas before they disappear. Reduce unnecessary cognitive load and alternate concentrated work with time for ideas to develop.

From an idea to a justified decision

Use these questions to examine a proposal, research finding or system. Distinguish what has been demonstrated from what still needs evidence.

Are we solving the right problem?

Clarify the need, affected people and alternatives before choosing a method. Ask whether AI adds value and whether the framing overlooks a simpler solution.

Does the measure match the goal?

Check whether the chosen metric represents the quality, benefit or harm you care about. A rising score can hide an unmet need or an unfair outcome.

What caused the change?

Distinguish the effect of an intervention from differences in data, conditions or timing. Compare alternatives and confounding explanations before making a causal claim.

Will the finding transfer?

Separate results on familiar examples from evidence on unseen data and different settings. Identify the boundaries within which a conclusion is supported.

When should we withhold judgement?

Record uncertainty and define when more evidence, abstention or human review is needed. Confidence should follow the evidence available for the particular claim.

Which trade-offs matter?

Consider accuracy, fairness, privacy, accessibility, latency and cost together. State whose interests and values shape the choice rather than hiding them in a metric.

What changes when parts interact?

Test interfaces and combined behaviour. Individually reliable components can produce failures through mismatched assumptions, feedback or unexpected dependencies.

How does the system change its world?

Look for feedback loops: decisions can alter behaviour and future data. Ask whether success today creates incentives or conditions that undermine tomorrow’s results.

What if conditions shift?

Consider changing users, environments, institutions and patterns. Decide which changes require fresh evidence instead of assuming an earlier result remains valid.

Can someone reproduce and maintain it?

Make methods, inputs, versions and reasoning traceable. Ask whether another person can repeat the result, understand its limits and repair or update the work.

Who can challenge or stop it?

Specify who reviews evidence, accepts risk, overrides decisions and suspends or retires a system. Consider affected people’s ability to understand, contest and recover from outcomes.

What would make us abandon it?

Set stopping criteria before becoming invested in an approach. Record evidence that would justify revision, restriction or choosing another solution.

Is the evidence sufficient for this use?

Match the strength of evidence to the consequences of error. A working demonstration or a completed schedule does not establish reliability, fairness or readiness for deployment.

Questions to use while thinking
  • What exactly am I trying to understand or decide?
  • What is observed, what is inferred and what is still unknown?
  • Which assumptions, perspectives or incentives may shape this account?
  • What are the strongest alternative explanations?
  • What would disconfirm my preferred explanation?
  • Who is affected, and what consequences have I overlooked?
  • How could I test the idea, and what would change my mind?
