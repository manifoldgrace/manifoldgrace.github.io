Human Life Domains, Missing Perspectives and AI Evaluation

Conceptual idea: AI development could benefit from better evaluations of how systems integrate evidence across ordinary human life, recognise underrepresented perspectives, distinguish uncertain claims from errors, and identify weaknesses in their own evaluation methods. This proposal is open to contributions from researchers, practitioners and members of the public; it is not a measure of any one person's knowledge or the current performance of a specific AI model.

2. Candidate model-evaluation limitations

These are research priorities, not assertions that every AI model fails at them. Each requires controlled testing and comparison with the strongest relevant research.

  • Longitudinal evidence: retain changes over time without replacing evidence with broad personal judgments.
  • Causal inference: separate what happened before an event from why it happened.
  • Cultural precision: separate language, ancestry, nationality, place of upbringing and self-description.
  • Practical and embodied knowledge: test advice against observed material constraints and outcomes.
  • Conflicting human values: preserve meaningful disagreements instead of manufacturing consensus.
  • Evaluator self-correction: test whether the system can recognise incorrect rubrics, missing categories and false exclusions.

3. Potentially underrepresented evidence types

Further investigation should seek counterexamples and not assume these areas are absent from all published research: everyday negotiation among people with different needs, documented repair-and-recovery episodes, oral and tacit knowledge, successful as well as unsuccessful institutional decisions, and unexpected questions that existing frameworks fail to ask.

4. Questions that contributors and researchers can test

These 29 candidate questions are proposed prompts for hypothesis formation, comparison and evidence collection; they are not validated findings. Proposed studies should specify comparator methods, provenance, uncertainty, success criteria and falsification conditions.

1. Longitudinal evidence and context

  1. How should a model distinguish a one-off event from a sustained pattern without making a character judgment?
  2. When does adding a historical record reverse a well-supported conclusion, and how should the revision be explained?
  3. How should missing or contradictory timelines affect confidence?
  4. What is a fair test of context retrieval when relevant evidence appears early, late or between unrelated material?

2. Causal reasoning and institutional outcomes

  1. Which evidence separates a documented cause from mere sequence or correlation?
  2. What alternative explanations must be ruled out before attributing a decision to bias or misconduct?
  3. Can an AI identify when a proposed intervention would change the outcome rather than just the explanation?
  4. What do successful institutional corrections reveal that failure-only samples miss?

3. Cultural and identity precision

  1. Can an AI keep citizenship, ancestry, lived upbringing, language and self-identification separate?
  2. When does an apparently accurate cultural category conceal important differences within the group?
  3. How does changing the language of the question alter the answer?
  4. Can the model ask for missing biographical information rather than infer identity from appearance or slang?

4. Practical and tacit knowledge

  1. How can firsthand craft or maintenance knowledge be tested against physical outcomes?
  2. Which information is lost when a practical procedure is summarised as abstract instructions?
  3. How can a model distinguish local expertise from a generally valid recommendation?
  4. What controlled real-world observations would disprove a confident but impractical answer?

5. Disagreement and value conflict

  1. When do conflicting annotations reflect genuine values rather than rater mistakes?
  2. Can competing values be represented accurately without forcing artificial consensus?
  3. How does a model explain a trade-off without imposing an unrequested moral ranking?
  4. How should evidence, preference and unresolved ambiguity be kept apart?

6. Evaluator fallibility and epistemic gaps

  1. Can a model flag instances where its own rubric, rather than an input, is defective?
  2. How many eventually validated ideas were rejected initially, and for what reason?
  3. Can independent reviewers identify underexplored questions that an AI failed to generate?
  4. Which uncertainty signals predict wrong acceptance, wrong rejection or overconfident silence?
  5. When do multiple-model reviews amplify the same mistaken assumption?

7. Missing perspectives and public knowledge

  1. Which domains of everyday cooperation lack representative benchmark cases?
  2. Which orally transmitted or locally held knowledge claims can be verified without exposing private people?
  3. What positive institutional outcomes are missing from failure-focused datasets?
  4. Can contributors supply counterexamples that alter an established evaluation category?

6. How others can contribute

Useful submissions could include: a precise question; a public or appropriately licensed evidence source; an anonymised counterexample; an alternative interpretation; a reproducible test; or a correction to an existing claim. State what is observed, what is inferred, what would disprove the claim, and whether any material can be shared publicly. Existing research enquiry route is available for proposals. This page does not automatically accept submissions, recruit study participants, or authorise use of submissions as AI training data.

Safeguards: do not submit private employment, medical, family or identifying records; seek consent where data relates to others. Public examples should be non-identifying, rights-cleared and suitably reviewed. An invitation to contribute is not a confirmed collaboration or a promise of publication.

8. Falsifiable research plan

Compare long-context, retrieval-assisted, independent multi-model and staged self-audit approaches on matched, non-sensitive tasks. Measure evidence recovery, temporal accuracy, causal identification, cultural category errors, disagreement preservation, legitimate-knowledge rejection, unsafe acceptance, cost and correction quality. Record null and negative results. A hypothesised improvement fails if it does not reproduce against the strongest relevant baseline, or violates privacy/safety criteria.
