RESEARCH PROPOSAL · AI · PERCEPTION · SOCIAL RESPONSIBILITY

Multimodal AI for Perception, Interpretation & Responsible Decision Support

Create art. Capture reality. Understand perception. Use evidence, alternative perspectives and explicit trade-offs to support sound human judgement—not to replace it.

Research question

Why do different people see different things in the same environment, event or image? How might multimodal AI clarify optical, ecological, biological, psychological, social and cultural influences on perception, while helping people access the evidence they need to make reflective, responsible decisions?

Some photographic frames appear more visually coherent or perceptually harmonious because of light, contrast, atmosphere, framing, timing and the observer's attention. This does not mean that the underlying place has become objectively more perfect.

Beauty, meaning and social responsibility

Beauty must not be reduced to attractiveness, attention capture or commercial appeal. Aesthetic experience may concern care, ecological richness, dignity, cultural meaning, craftsmanship, authenticity, belonging and the value of an environment to those who inhabit it. Representations of people and places can shape what audiences notice, neglect, protect or fund. Ethical visual communication therefore requires accurate context, respect for people and communities, and consideration of environmental consequences.

A compelling image is not necessarily a responsible image; a modest-looking scene may convey greater educational, social or ecological significance. ESG can help evaluate institutional impacts and accountability, but cannot supply a complete theory of aesthetics or human experience.

Harmony is not a single objective

Visual, ecological, social and economic harmony can complement or conflict with one another. An AI system should state which definition is being used, whose interests it reflects, and the consequences of optimising one consideration over another. More informed choice could contribute to a better-balanced society, but this is a testable aspiration rather than an established consequence.

Information, decision burden and wellbeing

Insufficient, conflicting or inaccessible information can make decisions more demanding and contribute to stress or uncertainty. That is not, by itself, a mental health diagnosis or a demonstrated cause of illness. The goal is not literally all information, which may be impossible or overwhelming, but sufficiently relevant, reliable, accessible and proportionate evidence for sound judgement. AI should identify unknowns, compare options, explain confidence and protect the user's right to disagree or defer a decision.

AI prototype and evaluation proposal

Compare alternative photographs of the same place under different optical conditions. Annotate observable characteristics separately from interpretations and social implications. Invite consenting participants with varied backgrounds to assess focal points and explanations. Test whether evidence mapping, alternatives and clarification improve comprehension, uncertainty calibration, autonomous decisions and responsible outcomes.

Potential evaluation measures: observation accuracy, provenance, fairness, accessibility, interpretive diversity, confidence calibration, cognitive load, decision comprehension, agency and environmental and social impact. A single numerical beauty score must not be treated as objective truth.
