What completeness can—and cannot—mean

This initial index contains 33 distinct frameworks, families or indices, grouped by their primary purpose. It is a curated starting inventory, not a claim that every framework in the world has been enumerated. Some are formal indices with published indicators; others are strategy tools or research models. Their names must not be treated as equivalent measurements.

A framework can be complete for its stated question while deliberately omitting other subjects. Consequently, the third column records illustrative scope limitations relative to an all-society ambition, not proven design defects. Formal comprehensiveness audits must inspect full methodology, indicator lists, data availability and actual practice before grading.

Research method: retain every framework's primary source, version, definition, unit of analysis, jurisdictions, time coverage, measurable dimensions, exclusions, contested assumptions, longitudinal comparability and correction history. Update this register as additional frameworks are discovered.

Proposed twelve-axis completeness audit

Evaluate each framework against the following researcher-proposed axes: human health and wellbeing; material security and housing; relationships and culture; education and capability; work, enterprise and financial mobility; rights and justice; governance and delivery; infrastructure and technology; ecology and resource stewardship; international interdependence; shocks and resilience; longitudinal continuity, implementation and accountability.

For each axis record: 0 not addressed, 1 acknowledged, 2 partially measured, 3 robustly operationalised, N/A outside design scope, ? not yet verified. These are provisional coding categories, not validated psychometric scores. Record explicit definitions, evidence links, uncertainty and reviewer disagreements before comparing frameworks. Missingness, double-counting and opposing effects must be visible.

Further audit lenses should include: person/household/community/institution/nation/world; current versus future; outcomes versus explanatory drivers; cross-domain interactions and second-order effects; state versus non-state action; distribution by affected population; rights versus discretionary preferences; and when non-aggregation is ethically necessary.

Potential original integrative frameworks

NATION

Needs and wellbeing; Agency and accountability; Trust and justice; Institutions and innovation; Opportunity and mobility; Nature and stewardship.

WORLD

Wellbeing; Order and cooperation; Resources and resilience; Learning and labour; Development and distribution.

CIVIC

Capability; Institutional integrity; Values and voice; Inclusion and implementation; Continuity, correction and consequences.

These acronyms are provisional labels; neither their uniqueness nor their completeness has been established. Prior-art searching must include academic repositories, policy literature, commercial usage and older terminology before any claim of originality.

Design principle: A universal-looking acronym will not itself 'cover everything'. A more credible research contribution may be a modular ontology and cross-framework evidence graph that can expand as new domains, institutions and values emerge, while showing unmeasured dimensions and conflicting assumptions.

Working-paper initiative

Provisional title: Beyond PESTLE: Mapping the Coverage, Omissions and Interdependencies of National and Global Assessment Frameworks.

Planned study: systematic framework discovery; eligibility and coding protocol; official methodology extraction; twelve-axis coverage matrix; overlap and contradiction analysis; sensitivity testing; stakeholder review; comparison of non-additive outcomes; and case studies contrasting apparent progress with lived conditions.

Research status: indexed literature and conceptual study design only. There is no completed systematic review, novel validated metric or published working-paper manuscript at this stage.
