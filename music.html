Key signatures, spellings & saxophone practice

Relative keys share a key signature: C major and A minor both have no sharps or flats. Parallel keys share a tonic: C major and C minor have different signatures. The minor signature shown is for natural minor; harmonic and melodic minor alter notes beyond it.

Enharmonic spellings: F♯ major and G♭ major sound alike in equal temperament but have six sharps and six flats respectively. C♯ and D♭ have seven sharps and five flats; C♭ and B have seven flats and five sharps. Letter spelling matters when reading notation.

Ten-minute practice: name the signature, play the major scale slowly, then its relative natural minor. Arpeggiate I, IV and V, listen to the return to I, then repeat in a neighbouring key. Increase tempo only after tone, fingering and rhythm are secure.

Some parallel minors (D♭, G♭ and C♭ minor) require theoretical signatures with double flats. Their enharmonic alternatives (C♯, F♯ and B minor) are usually easier to read.

Concert pitch: the audio is in concert pitch. For B♭ tenor or soprano saxophone, the written key is a major second above the concert key; for E♭ alto or baritone, it is a major sixth above. For example, concert C major is written D major for B♭ instruments and A major for E♭ instruments; sounding octave also depends on the instrument.

Self-check: cover the result and name the relative minor, parallel minor and accidentals before revealing them again. Then explain why a move to the dominant changes just one note in a major scale.

Music, spirituality & worship

Musical training can support attentive worship: listening to others, preparing faithfully, choosing singable keys and serving the congregation through clear, sensitive ensemble playing.

Psalm 150:3–5 includes instruments in praise; Colossians 3:16 connects psalms, hymns and spiritual songs with teaching and gratitude; 1 Corinthians 14:26 places edification at the centre of gathered contributions. Read these passages in context alongside the Faith page's account of music and service.

The Circle of Fifths describes musical relationships. Reflections on worship and Christian formation are a separate area of study; no particular key signature determines spiritual maturity or God's presence.
