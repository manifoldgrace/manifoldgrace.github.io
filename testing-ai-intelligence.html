Testing AI Intelligence: Depth, Breadth & Context

An AI can give a fluent answer without exploring every important possibility. How can an ordinary reader tell whether an answer is short, wide-ranging, deeply explained, factually sound or actually complete?

The central idea: Do not judge an AI answer only by how much it writes or how confidently it sounds. Check what it covers (breadth), how well it explains (depth), whether it understands the question and history (context), and whether its claims withstand verification (accuracy and evidence). This page explains a proposed testing approach, not a completed intelligence ranking.

1. What do these words mean?

2. What does a typical AI answer look like?

Many conversational answers are organised into an opening sentence, a few grouped explanations and a concluding takeaway. The following is an illustrative format, not a measured claim about the majority of AI answers on the internet.

Response componentIllustrative appearanceWhat it demonstratesWhat it does not prove
OpeningOne or two sentences stating the answer.Clarity and relevance.That alternative conclusions have been examined.
Grouped pointsThree to six bullet points or short paragraphs.Organisation and selected coverage.That all distinct possibilities were considered.
TableSeveral rows naming concepts and giving short descriptions.Compact comparisons.That each row is a deep or independent explanation.

ConclusionA summarising sentence or next step.Closure and usability.That additional investigation would find nothing new.

Three answers to the same question

Example question: “Why might an AI omit important ideas?”

Level A — Label only

“Premature convergence; limited context retrieval; default brevity.” These are three terms, not three developed explanations.

Level B — Brief explanation

“The model may prioritise short, relevant-looking answers. That can leave out less obvious interpretations. Also, information supplied earlier in a long conversation may not be used reliably.” This provides mechanisms but little evidence or testing.

Level C — Developed explanation

“An assistant may correctly identify the most obvious interpretation of a question and compose a coherent answer around it. But an open-ended question can have several defensible frames: a technical cause, a contextual misunderstanding, a missing source or a hidden assumption. If only one frame is examined, the response can appear complete despite missing alternatives. A rigorous test would supply the same question to multiple conditions, compare their distinct valid ideas against an independently constructed reference set, and see whether targeted follow-up questions recover previously omitted material. Such recovery would demonstrate an omission in the initial response; it would not by itself reveal exactly which internal mechanism caused it.”

4. How to test AI answers fairly

Step 1 — Choose the same questions. Include factual, open-ended, ethical, technical and interdisciplinary questions. Define what a good answer should accomplish before seeing model outputs.

Step 2 — Control the conditions. Keep question wording, available sources, time allowance and permitted tools comparable. Record model/version and test date.

Step 3 — Vary the requested depth and breadth. Compare a default answer, 10 alternatives, 30 alternatives, up to 100 alternatives and an evidence-first iterative approach. Do not assume the largest number is best.

Step 4 — Count distinct, valid ideas. Code repetitions separately from new mechanisms, hypotheses and counterarguments. Use more than one independent reviewer and resolve disagreements transparently.

Step 5 — Evaluate depth separately. Ask whether each central idea includes an explanation, an example, supporting evidence, limits and implications. Word count is recorded but is not a proxy for quality.

Step 6 — Check omissions and corrections. Compare answers with independently assembled references. Provide targeted prompts about missing areas and record whether the AI can recover them.

Step 7 — Compare with humans fairly. Match expertise, access to sources, task definitions and relevant time limits. Report variation across individuals, tasks and models; do not infer universal superiority from a few examples.

Step 8 — Publish uncertainty. Distinguish direct observations, provisional hypotheses and replicated findings. Share the rubric, anonymised examples and limitations before making general claims.

5. A simple scoring guide

Each dimension can be rated 0–4 using a published rubric. The scores are proposed measurements; they have not been validated as a general intelligence scale.

ScoreReader-friendly meaningEvidence needed
0Absent, incorrect or not assessable.No useful evidence for the dimension.
1Named or asserted only.A term or claim without an adequate explanation.
2Basic explanation.Relevant description with limited support or alternatives.
3Developed explanation.Mechanism, example, evidence and meaningful limitations.
4Well-substantiated and critically tested.Verified evidence, relevant counterarguments and justified uncertainty.

Important: a score of 4 on a narrow task is not proof of general intelligence or superintelligence. Breadth, depth, context and correctness should be reported as separate results, not reduced to one impressive number without validation.

7. Research questions still open

Does a system stop exploring because of instructions, optimisation for usefulness, lack of evidence or limitations in its ability to identify unexplored categories? Can we design a test that distinguishes a genuinely new explanation from a repeated one? Is there a measurable point of diminishing returns as a model moves from 10 to 30 to 100 possibilities? Does corrective questioning improve answers because it adds information, changes priorities or prompts a more extensive search? These are empirical questions, not settled conclusions.

This topic belongs to the Integrated Societal Intelligence programme and complements the existing advanced AI evaluation protocol and qualitative LLM evaluation record. The proposed novel contribution is a joint breadth–depth–context–evidence audit that measures valid alternatives, explanatory development and recoverable omissions under comparable test conditions. Whether this combination is original requires a more comprehensive literature overlap audit.
