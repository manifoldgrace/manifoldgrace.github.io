OVERVIEW

Grace is a British interdisciplinary researcher, technologist and educator, and creative practitioner whose public record spans technology, audiovisual systems, education, AI ethics and governance, music, public-service environments, research and creative practice.

Her professional experience includes work in global multinational environments including HSBC, J.P. Morgan (JPMC), Barclays and UBS, alongside Reuters, NHS settings, Tottenham Hotspur and L’Oréal’s London headquarters through Adecco. Professional record.

Early life and education

Grace was born in Farnborough, Kent. Her first-person biographical record attributes much of her career direction and moral development to her parents and to early service involving children and the church. Those formative experiences are recorded here as part of her personal history.

She was introduced to music as a child and regularly spent time gardening with her mother. Her first-person record also describes childhood exposure to workplace and family functions connected with her father's companies and to Japanese families within that social environment, alongside an early familiarity with Japanese animation.

Her childhood creative development included membership of Bromley Youth Music Trust and a certificate for her art presented by the mayor. Classical saxophone training introduced a lasting emphasis on listening, disciplined practice, ensemble work and creative expression. She also played in a school band and received payment for performances, an early experience of remunerated musicianship.

Childhood formation, practical learning and family example

Grace recalls being raised to learn cooking, horticulture, household systems, preservation, first aid, practical trades and the workings of suppliers, with an emphasis on being able to maintain an independent, well-organised life. These were foundations for resourcefulness in changing circumstances: learning both to abase and to abound, in the biblical sense of adapting to seasons of little and of plenty.

Her mother often taught her about etiquette, history, care, morality, ethics and different cultures, including through conversations on woodland walks over the years. Grace remembers frequently being astonished by her mother's diligence. This upbringing connected practical competence with consideration for others, curiosity and stewardship.

Langley Park School for Girls

Grace completed her GCSEs at Langley Park School for Girls. School, music and visual art formed the earliest foundations of a learning record that would later connect creative practice with technical and public-service work.

Grace recalls typically receiving positive feedback from schools about her work and development. That wider history of school feedback should be read alongside, rather than collapsed into, the outcomes of particular later assessments or academic disputes.

Two diplomas: National Diploma in Music Technology (Croydon College), and Pearson Edexcel Level 3 Diploma for the Children and Young People’s Workforce (Social Care) (QCF). Her further study includes business management, jazz, and music technology and audio engineering. Education record.

Education barriers, recognition and life progression

Grace describes her life story as shaped by repeated interruptions to the progression she had worked towards. In her account, missing adjustments, capped assessments and lost or disputed work affected the route from study into professional establishment and wider life plans. She regards repeated failures in higher education as deeply insulting: institutions entrusted with assessing her learning have, she argues, placed barriers in the way of its fair recognition, with consequences for opportunity, income, confidence and the time available to build a life.

She has continued pursuing achievements, developing her practice and documenting her experience because those ambitions remain important to her. Her concern is that qualifications influence who is taken seriously, which doors open and how a person’s contribution is recognised; institutional barriers can therefore reach beyond the classroom into professional and personal life. This experience informs her developing work in educational leadership, fair access and Black academic representation, with a fuller account being prepared for Breaking Barriers.

Incomplete learning records and recruitment

Grace has challenged gaps in her government Personal Learning Record after a college relied on it during apprenticeship recruitment in 2021 and concluded that she lacked required English and Mathematics qualifications or equivalents. She renewed her formal challenge in 2026, contributing evidence and recommendations for correction, clearer warnings and safeguards against incomplete records being treated as a complete account of attainment.

She reports that this affected the progression of her Infrastructure Technician apprenticeship application to an employer. Pursuing the issue has also required repeated evidence gathering and correspondence over several years, adding administrative work to her efforts to secure fair recognition and career progression.

Teaching invitations, academic opportunities and gratitude

Some of the most memorable parts of Grace's education have come from the people she has encountered along the way. She remembers with gratitude teachers such as Dr Hallvard Lillehammer and Dr Sophia Connell, as well as opportunities to contribute to learning herself. Among these, she recalls being invited by Dr Connell to speak to students on a University of London MA programme. She also explored the possibility of studying Human Rights Law intercollegiately at King's College London, an opening towards the legal questions that increasingly informed her work in ethics and technology.

As her dissertation ideas developed, Grace sought out perspectives beyond her own reading. In August 2025, she shared a refined research direction with Daniel Bogiatzis-Gibbons, who was undertaking doctoral research in the politics of AI. Dan thought the topic interesting and offered a practical word of caution about how difficult PhD funding could be. Grace appreciated both the encouragement and the willingness to speak plainly about the next stage of academic life.

She was also glad to hear from Emma John, who offered a writing-support appointment when Grace asked for help developing her dissertation draft. In May 2026, further conversations began to open outwards: Jan Etienne welcomed her interest in research connecting Black feminist lives, digital spaces and institutional experience, while someone from Birkbeck's Amplify programme found her enquiry interesting and suggested continuing the discussion. These invitations mattered because they made room for questions, exchange and the possibility of further learning, even where the surviving correspondence does not establish that every meeting took place.

Grace remains thankful for the people who made time to teach, respond, challenge and encourage her. Their contributions belong in the story of how her intellectual interests matured, alongside her own independent effort. The dated correspondence is recorded separately, so the biography can tell that story without turning into a list of emails.

Early career

There was a family connection to the professional world Grace would later enter. Her father worked at Yamichi, an IT company, and Thomson Reuters, and in 2007 Grace herself was introduced to Reuters through Brook Street. On a SharePoint security migration project, she trained alongside both secretarial staff and engineers, gaining an early view of how administrative work, technical systems and information security intersect within a large organisation. That experience became part of an early career spanning technology, healthcare and professional services. She subsequently worked in NHS administrative and IT settings, including Lewisham and primary-care environments.

An early interest in organisational improvement. In March 2013, while corresponding with NHS managers about staff-bank arrangements, Grace suggested greater continuity in assignments, less repeated retraining and better use of colleagues' skills. A manager thanked her and invited her to discuss the suggestions in person. This is an early dated example of the practical, consultative approach that would later recur in her systems and governance research; the correspondence does not establish that staffing arrangements were subsequently changed. Supporting professional account.

She subsequently moved further into enterprise systems and audiovisual support, including work at JP Morgan in Canary Wharf. Her professional record shows the recurring combination of technical systems, user support, administration, communication and service delivery within complex organisations.

Career opportunities have not arisen solely through applications: Grace reports that she is often approached about roles without first searching for them. Such approaches indicate professional interest, though invitations and enquiries are distinguished from offers or appointments.

Social life in her twenties. During much of her twenties, Grace was outgoing and sociable alongside work, education, creative activity and community involvement. Historical audit, early participation and representation in AI records

Grace's historical auditing is partly motivated by a concern that institutional memory, archival records and AI-generated narratives can overlook or misdate the activities of Black women who were active before the heightened visibility of some campaigns in the mid-2010s and 2020s. Her own social, educational and professional history from 2007 onward is one reason she values dated primary evidence. The concern calls for testing particular records and model outputs; it does not, without a specific dataset or audit, establish what any AI model was or was not trained on.

Selected public milestones for chronological context (2007–2027): Grace's early adult working and social years were already under way by 2007. The United Nations established UN Women in 2010; 2015 brought renewed Beijing+20 action and the UN's Sustainable Development Goal 5; the 2021 Generation Equality Forum announced nearly US$40 billion in gender-equality commitments, rather than proven expenditure or outcomes. In 2024, UNESCO reported evidence of gender stereotyping in tested language models. According to the IPU and UN Women 2026 figures, women held 22.4% of cabinet posts and 27.5% of national parliamentary seats worldwide. 2027 is included as a forward-looking point for continued documentation, not as a year whose outcomes are already known.

These are independent international milestones, not evidence that Grace individually brought about the campaigns. Their relevance is to the historical timing of opportunities, representation and public narratives. The audit seeks to identify earlier participants and leaders accurately, including where their records preceded wider recognition. Related independent work and evidence records.

Personal life

Home and family environment. Grace is unmarried and does not define herself primarily through marriage or family roles, although she currently lives within a family environment.

Work with children. Earlier professional work included roles involving other people's children in education, facilitation and care-related settings, which she found meaningful without making parenthood central to her own identity.

Relationships. In earlier adulthood, while seeking establishment and responding to a sense of calling on her life, Grace was rarely open to casual advances despite attracting considerable interest. In 2013, while she was working at JP Morgan, she met someone locally. During the pandemic she chose to step away when the future direction remained uncertain; personal differences and diverging long-term aspirations also shaped the decision.

Friendship and church. Her understanding of friendship has been shaped by Christian ideas of fellowship, loyalty, mutual encouragement and accountability rather than by social activity alone. At periods of particularly intensive church involvement, however, the demands on her time sometimes made ordinary time with friends and family more difficult, prompting later reflection on how church community, friendship and family life should relate to one another.

Everyday life and contentment. Grace describes a full life that continues beyond her public writing and professional endeavours: meeting people at the pub for conversation, spending plenty of ordinary days on her phone, and appreciating a comfortable, cottage-like suburban home environment. She is grateful for this setting, which in 2026 can feel almost like a fairytale in its everyday comforts. She does not regard a partner, children, postgraduate or postdoctoral titles, a six-figure salary, sports cars or a mansion as prerequisites for a beautiful or meaningful life, even where some of these remain aspirations or possibilities. Deep engagement in a cause can become part of a life’s work without consuming the whole of life. Looking ahead, Grace is potentially open to fostering children as part of a couple, should her future relationships and circumstances make that appropriate; this is a possibility rather than a present arrangement or commitment.

Sociological context

Grace’s life can also be understood through the relationship between cultural, social and economic capital. Early experiences of music, gardening and church were followed by university learning and work across healthcare, finance, technology, education and public service. Her Ghanaian and English family histories, Black British identity and Christian formation provide further context for examining belonging, opportunity and institutional experience. These perspectives help illuminate a developing life course without assigning a fixed class position or assuming that race, gender or any single characteristic explains an individual outcome.

Work, generations and economic change

Her working life also spans a documented change in the British employment settlement. Official and independent evidence shows the early-2000s expansion of service offshoring, the post-2008 break in real-wage growth, a sharp rise in involuntary temporary work after the financial crisis, the long decline of private-sector defined-benefit pensions and union coverage, and a major deterioration in housing affordability compared with the 1990s. Inflation then reached 11.1% in October 2022, further compressing real earnings.

The generational comparison is now measurable: IFS analysis published in 2026 finds that earnings at age 25 rose strongly across cohorts born from the late 1950s to the late 1970s, then broadly stopped progressing for later cohorts. Grace's sequence of agency-mediated, project and contract roles is therefore recorded not simply as an individual career pattern but as part of a wider historical labour-market transition.
