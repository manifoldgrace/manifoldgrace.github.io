FOUNDATIONAL RESEARCH PRINCIPLE

The Open Frontier of Knowledge and Innovation

Research and engineering developments may exist beyond the knowledge represented in an AI system's training data, accessible sources or current retrieval capabilities. This includes unpublished, privately developed, newly emerging or otherwise unrepresented work.

An AI system's inability to identify an advancement does not establish that it does not exist, or that a known approach represents the highest achievable level of scientific or technological development.

Frontier inquiry therefore requires epistemic openness: recognising the limits of available evidence, considering alternative architectures and independent discoveries, examining unfamiliar claims critically, and updating conclusions when new evidence becomes available.

Important distinction: assume that knowledge may be incomplete; do not assume that any particular unverified breakthrough exists. Openness to discovery should coexist with rigorous evaluation, uncertainty reporting and appropriate safety controls.

MY RESEARCH AVENUES · MA FOUNDATIONS · 5 NOVEMBER 2026

Urgent research gaps: developing my MA foundations

These ten avenues form part of my independent research, developing from my 2024–25 study of AI, Ethics and Society at Birkbeck, University of London. They connect technical mechanisms with philosophical questions, social research, institutional practice and the consequences of deployment. They sit within the wider arena index below; they are not ten new, separate project pages.

The programme specification combines technical knowledge with philosophical and ethical inquiry, case analysis, policy development, communication and independent research. The History and Foundations of AI syllabus supplies the more specific connections identified here. Specialist applications extend those foundations; they are not all named subjects in the taught syllabus.

  1. Real-world AI reliability and evaluation

    Research gap: Establish when test performance transfers to unfamiliar inputs, incomplete evidence and changing operational conditions. Investigate calibration, abstention, reproducibility and the difference between correct output and justified conclusions.

    MA foundation: Meeting 4, probabilities and prediction; Meeting 6, feedback loops and model collapse; Meeting 9, explainability and accountability. Existing laboratory and experiment records →

  2. Autonomous agents, cybersecurity and human control

    Research gap: Test permissions, resistance to malicious instructions, recovery after errors and the effectiveness of human intervention when agents act through tools.

    MA foundation: Meeting 3, artificial advisers and human–AI interaction; Meeting 9, design and accountability. Cybersecurity and agent-control engineering are further technical extensions. Existing agent architecture study →

  3. Evidence integrity, provenance and dependent revision

    Research gap: Distinguish independent corroboration from repeated reporting; preserve conditions and source ancestry; update affected conclusions when a premise changes while retaining historical records.

    MA foundation: Meetings 1–2, intelligence, meaning and rules; Meeting 5, data; Meeting 6, recursion; Meeting 9, accountability. Provenance systems and revision benchmarks develop these connections. Existing evidence architecture →

  4. Institutional accountability and effective oversight

    Research gap: Determine which review, challenge, escalation and correction arrangements actually protect people affected by automated decisions, and where responsibility becomes dispersed.

    MA foundation: Meeting 7, AI, power and the state; Meeting 8, unfairness and alternative approaches; Meeting 9, accountability. The programme also includes developing and critiquing AI policy and regulation. Existing systems and institutional research →

  5. Healthcare AI, evidence quality and research ethics

    Research gap: Examine evidence standards, sensitive-data use, unequal performance and oversight before findings or tools are relied upon in health settings.

    MA foundation: The programme's cross-domain ethical analysis, case-study and applied-framework outcomes; Meeting 9, accountable design. Clinical validation requires additional health-methods expertise and is an application beyond the named syllabus themes. Existing cross-sector evidence applications →

  6. Employment, income distribution and beneficial automation

    Research gap: Measure changes to work, income, entry routes and bargaining power; distinguish potential task exposure from observed displacement, and investigate how gains can be shared.

    MA foundation: Meeting 5, AI data work and data–compute–labour relationships; Meeting 7, power and inequality; Meeting 10, human welfare and material consequences. Existing labour-market research →

  7. Energy, computing efficiency and physical infrastructure

    Research gap: Investigate resource measurement, efficient computation and the environmental and distributional consequences of AI infrastructure. Distinguish scientific uncertainties from grid, procurement and construction delays.

    MA foundation: Meeting 10, the materiality of AI; Meeting 5, data, compute and labour. Hardware optimisation and power-system engineering are specialist extensions. Existing platform-design reference →

  8. Language, culture and unequal model performance

    Research gap: Test interpretation and reliability beyond dominant languages and well-represented cultural settings; investigate whose knowledge and experience are omitted from data and evaluation.

    MA foundation: Meeting 2, grounded cognition; Meeting 5, data and values; Meeting 8, fairness and counter-imaginaries; Meeting 10, colonial power. Existing research and culture study →

  9. Scientific discovery and independent verification

    Research gap: Establish whether AI-assisted literature analysis, hypotheses and experiments survive independent checking and improve research outcomes, rather than merely generating plausible material.

    MA foundation: Meeting 1, thinking machines; Meeting 4, prediction; Meeting 6, feedback loops; the programme's independent-research outcome. Domain-specific scientific validation is a further methodological extension. Existing evaluation protocol →

  10. Public communication, ambiguity and consequential omissions

    Research gap: Test whether assisted review preserves qualifications, identifies foreseeable misleading interpretations and improves readers' understanding without inventing intent or suppressing legitimate uncertainty.

    MA foundation: Meeting 2, rules and meaning; Meeting 3, AI interfaces; Meeting 9, design and accountability; the programme's communication outcome for diverse audiences. Existing semantic-integrity study →

DEEPER RESEARCH · EXPLORATORY PROJECT DESIGN · 5 NOVEMBER 2026

Nine avenues for investigating persistent AI bottlenecks

I intend to explore these avenues more deeply, potentially creating bounded projects to understand the bottlenecks and then write about their mechanisms, evidence and practical consequences. They extend the MA-connected research directions above. Each project below is a possible investigation, not an experiment already completed.

These questions concern capabilities that are not reliably established across demanding conditions, rather than blanket claims of impossibility or assumptions about developers' motives. Existing studies supply starting points; findings must be interpreted within their tested models, tasks and dates.

  1. Continual learning

    Bottleneck to investigate: Acquiring new knowledge while retaining earlier knowledge and reusing experience appropriately; retrieval, conversation memory and changes to model weights must be evaluated separately.

    Possible exploratory project: Build a small fictional fact stream with scheduled corrections. Compare fresh context, retained conversation and an external memory store; measure retention, useful transfer and obsolete-answer rates. Weight updates can be a later, separately resourced experiment.

    Writing focus: Explain when apparent learning is retrieval, what changes persist and where adaptation creates forgetting or interference.

    Research starting point: Continual Learning in Large Language Models (2026) →

  2. Machine unlearning

    Bottleneck to investigate: Distinguishing removal of a target influence from refusal, temporary suppression or damage to unrelated capabilities; a recovery test can itself introduce the information again.

    Possible exploratory project: Using a small open model and fictional data, compare refusal instructions with a documented unlearning method. Separate low-information recovery probes from retraining, and test retained-task performance. A refusal-only pilot can study evaluation design without claiming deletion.

    Writing focus: Document what each test establishes about removal, suppression, recoverability and utility; state the model and system boundary.

    Research starting point: Existing Large Language Model Unlearning Evaluations Are Inconclusive →

  3. Multi-agent collaboration

    Bottleneck to investigate: Coordination, shared assumptions, evidence transmission and termination can offset the benefits of role specialisation. Agreement between agents does not establish independent verification.

    Possible exploratory project: Compare one agent with a small agent team on the same fictional evidence tasks, under matched total token or cost budgets. Log handoff losses, unsupported agreement, recovery and completion.

    Writing focus: Analyse when collaboration improves results, when orchestration introduces errors and which failures are attributable to shared evidence rather than team size.

    Research starting point: MAST: Why Do Multi-Agent LLM Systems Fail? →

  4. Long-horizon computer operation

    Bottleneck to investigate: Sustaining state, recognising changes and recovering across multi-application workflows; a successful short demonstration does not establish dependable autonomy.

    Possible exploratory project: Construct a sandbox workflow using fictional documents and a mock interface. Vary step count, introduce changed instructions and recoverable errors, and record completion, unintended actions, interventions and time.

    Writing focus: Identify which task dependencies cause breakdown, how errors accumulate and whether recovery mechanisms improve end-to-end outcomes.

    Research starting point: OSWorld 2.0 research and benchmark →

  5. Robotics and embodied intelligence

    Bottleneck to investigate: Translating instructions and visual observations into spatially correct actions, with planning and feedback under physical constraints.

    Possible exploratory project: Begin in a simulator with a small navigation or manipulation task. Vary object positions, occlusion or instructions; separate perception, planning and execution errors. Physical deployment would require a further safety and validation stage.

    Writing focus: Explain where language-level competence fails to transfer to action, including the limits of simulation-to-reality conclusions.

    Research starting point: EmbodiedBench →

  6. Physically faithful world models

    Bottleneck to investigate: Visual plausibility is different from faithful physical prediction; consistency must be checked against measurable relationships rather than appearance alone.

    Possible exploratory project: Choose one simple physical system, such as a pendulum or collision, and compare generated predictions against a controlled simulation. Define measurement tolerances and distinguish observation-extraction errors from physical inconsistencies.

    Writing focus: Describe which relationships survive prediction, where they fail and why visual quality alone is an inadequate validation measure.

    Research starting point: WorldModelBench →

  7. Reasoning under increasing complexity

    Bottleneck to investigate: Additional reasoning tokens or search may help only under particular conditions; task complexity, output limits and verification quality can confound conclusions.

    Possible exploratory project: Create automatically checkable rule or planning problems with controlled depth. Vary the available reasoning budget while recording accuracy, truncation, latency and error type; keep task content and scoring fixed.

    Writing focus: Map where extra computation helps, plateaus or harms performance without treating one failure curve as proof that reasoning is impossible.

    Research starting point: The Illusion of Thinking: complexity-controlled research →

  8. End-to-end software engineering

    Bottleneck to investigate: Producing plausible code is different from diagnosing a system, satisfying requirements and making a change that survives integration and regression checks.

    Possible exploratory project: Use a small versioned repository with seeded defects and independent acceptance tests. Compare patch generation with diagnosis-plus-testing workflows; inspect regressions and requirement coverage rather than code appearance.

    Writing focus: Investigate the gap between a locally passing patch and a correctly completed engineering task, including the limitations of the test suite itself.

    Research starting point: SWE-Lancer →

  9. Efficient reasoning on smaller hardware

    Bottleneck to investigate: Lower precision or smaller models can change accuracy, generation length and recovery needs; cheaper individual operations need not yield cheaper successful tasks.

    Possible exploratory project: Compare two supported precision settings on a modest open model and fixed task set. Measure answer quality, peak memory, wall-clock time and generated tokens on the same hardware; report hardware constraints and avoid inferring energy use without measuring it.

    Writing focus: Analyse quality–resource trade-offs using cost or time per successful task, rather than model size or per-token speed alone.

    Research starting point: Quantization Inflates Reasoning (2026) →

Project method: define the claim and system boundary; compare a defensible baseline; control resources and confounders; retain failures and source versions; distinguish observations from explanations. Detailed results belong with the relevant project and existing laboratory records, rather than being repeated across this index.
