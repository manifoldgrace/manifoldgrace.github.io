RESEARCH / HCI / PSYCHOMETRIC METHOD

Human Capability Benchmarking Experiment

A structured experiment for separating three different kinds of evidence: long-run behavioural observations from human–AI interaction, published population or clinical-research benchmarks, and future task scores collected under defined conditions. It is not a diagnosis and does not treat conversational impressions as psychometric results.

METHOD FIRST

How a higher-or-lower conclusion will be earned

The experiment will not label a capability “above average” or “below average” from opinion alone. Each measurable capability needs an operational task, a suitable comparison sample and a numerical result. Where a test supplies age-adjusted standard scores, those are preferred. Where only raw population data exist, the source population, age range, mean or median, dispersion and direction of better performance are recorded before comparison.

Standard score
Common index convention: mean 100, SD 15.
Scaled score
Common subtest convention: mean 10, SD 3.
T-score
Common convention: mean 50, SD 10.
Percentile
P50 is the median; P84 is about +1 SD for a normal distribution.

Experimental classification rule: below P16 = lower than the reference mean by roughly one standard deviation or more; P16–P84 = broad reference range; above P84 = higher than the reference mean by roughly one standard deviation or more. Source-provided percentile cut-offs take priority where the distribution is skewed. “Mode” is retained when a source reports it, but many continuous cognitive datasets do not publish a useful modal value; median/IQR is often more appropriate for skewed timing or error data.

SPACE-SAVING TEST PLAN

Candidate tests to run

These are non-diagnostic research tasks. Where a proprietary clinical instrument is the appropriate comparator, the site will not reproduce protected test items; it will instead use an original analogue or recommend formal administration by a qualified professional if a clinical interpretation is needed.

Memory battery

Immediate recall, delayed recall, working-memory updating, visual-location memory and prospective memory. Record raw score, retention percentage and confidence.

Reaction & processing speed

Simple and choice reaction-time trials, using median latency, error rate and within-person variability.

Attention & inhibition

Visual search, sustained target detection, go/no-go and a colour-word interference analogue.

Switching & flexibility

Alternating-rule and number/letter sequencing tasks; measure switch cost and errors.

Verbal fluency & categorisation

Timed semantic and letter-generation tasks plus category-boundary judgements; compare only with compatible norms.

Reasoning

Original verbal, numerical, abstract and visual-spatial items with difficulty calibration across repeated sets.

Planning & prioritisation

Constraint-based scheduling and minimum-move puzzles. Score feasibility, efficiency and unnecessary steps.

Error detection

Review a controlled document or system description containing seeded inconsistencies; score detection and correction.

Metacognition

After each task, estimate confidence. Compare confidence with actual performance to calculate calibration error.

Creative association

Alternative uses, reframing and remote-association-style original items. Separate idea quantity from novelty and usefulness.

Social reasoning

Short scenarios with incomplete information; score perspective-taking, uncertainty handling and avoidance of unsupported motive claims.

Work-style longitudinal measures

Track planned versus actual duration, start latency, interruptions, completion, recovery after setbacks and learning slope across real projects.

Peripheral considerations: what the scores can and cannot predict

Cognitive performance can be relevant to training and job performance, but no single score determines employability, business success or the “right” way of life. A 2026 review of contemporary meta-analytic evidence reported modest correlations between general mental ability and overall job performance in newer datasets, substantially below older headline estimates. Entrepreneurship research likewise finds associations between some personality patterns and entrepreneurial status or outcomes, but individual effects are generally small and heterogeneous.

Employment outcomes also reflect opportunity, health, education, discrimination, labour-market conditions, social capital, accommodations, finance, sector choice and employer practice. UK official statistics therefore belong beside — not inside — the cognitive table. For example, the Buckland Review reported that around 3 in 10 working-age autistic people were in employment, compared with around 5 in 10 disabled people overall and 8 in 10 non-disabled people; that is an outcome statistic, not a cognitive-performance norm.

Accordingly, high scores may support hypotheses about particular work demands; lower scores may identify support needs, compensating strengths or environments that fit better. Neither establishes a person's worth, career ceiling or entitlement to education.

Observed interaction evidence: hypothesis layer only

Longitudinal conversations can provide ecological evidence about behaviours such as sustained research, error correction, conceptual association, planning, reframing and persistence. They are valuable because they occur across real tasks, but they are not standardized conditions. This layer will therefore be stored separately from numerical psychometric results and will never be used to manufacture a percentile.

Age, education, language and test conditions

Norms can change materially with age, education, language, device, sleep, fatigue, practice effects and testing environment. A valid comparison must use the correct reference group and record the conditions under which the task was completed. Adult-only norms will not be used to classify a younger participant, and older-adult cohort data will not be treated as a universal population average.

Sources & benchmark notes
  • UKB — UK Biobank cognitive data. A published analysis of 480,416 participants aged roughly 40–70 reported numeric-memory mean 6.70 (SD 1.34), verbal-numerical-reasoning mean 5.99 (SD 2.16), reaction-time median 546 ms (IQR 130), visual-memory errors median 3 (IQR 3), and 93.9% success on the prospective-memory item. Open paper ↗
  • Murphy — adult lifespan executive-function norms. UK neuropsychology researchers published age-banded Stroop, letter-fluency and Hayling norms across ages 18–92. For example, the 35–49 group had mean Stroop correct 119.25 (SD 20.71) and COWAT-S mean 17.10 (SD 4.78); the paper provides percentile tables for each age band. Open paper ↗
  • ANDI — Advanced Neuropsychological Diagnostics Infrastructure. Aggregated control datasets include Trail Making A/B, letter and semantic fluency, Stroop, delayed verbal and visual recall, coding and other neuropsychological measures across large pooled samples. Open database paper ↗
  • UK category-production norms. UK researchers published semantic-category production data for 117 concrete and abstract categories, useful for categorisation and semantic-fluency research. Open paper ↗
  • CANTAB normative work. Cambridge Cognition reports adult normative samples of around 750 participants per collection block across episodic memory, emotion recognition, executive functioning, working memory and other domains, with age-related effects. Summary ↗
  • ACS / Wechsler score conventions. Pearson's Advanced Clinical Solutions documentation describes scaled scores with mean 10/SD 3, index scores with mean 100/SD 15, and T-scores with mean 50/SD 10. Manual chapter ↗
  • ONS — socioeconomic outcomes, not psychometric norms. ONS 2021 disability outcomes reported employment 53.5% for disabled versus 81.6% for non-disabled people, with autism at 29.0% among respondents reporting it as their main impairment; degree attainment was 24.9% versus 42.7%. ONS release ↗
  • Buckland Review of Autism Employment. The 2024 government-backed review reports around 3 in 10 working-age autistic people in employment versus about 5 in 10 disabled people overall and 8 in 10 non-disabled people. GOV.UK report ↗
  • Job-performance interpretation. A 2026 review of newer and older meta-analytic evidence reports that general mental ability predicts job performance, but contemporary corrected estimates are modest and vary with job characteristics. Journal article ↗
  • Entrepreneurship interpretation. Zhao & Seibert's meta-analysis found small individual Big Five differences between entrepreneurs and managers, with a moderate multivariate relationship across the set of traits (R=.37). PubMed record ↗
