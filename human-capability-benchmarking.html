RESEARCH / HCI / PSYCHOMETRIC METHOD

Human Capability Benchmarking Experiment

A structured experiment for separating three different kinds of evidence: long-run behavioural observations from human–AI interaction, published population or clinical-research benchmarks, and future task scores collected under defined conditions. It is not a diagnosis and does not treat conversational impressions as psychometric results.

METHOD FIRST

How a higher-or-lower conclusion will be earned

The experiment will not label a capability “above average” or “below average” from opinion alone. Each measurable capability needs an operational task, a suitable comparison sample and a numerical result. Where a test supplies age-adjusted standard scores, those are preferred. Where only raw population data exist, the source population, age range, mean or median, dispersion and direction of better performance are recorded before comparison.

Standard score
Common index convention: mean 100, SD 15.
Scaled score
Common subtest convention: mean 10, SD 3.
T-score
Common convention: mean 50, SD 10.
Percentile
P50 is the median; P84 is about +1 SD for a normal distribution.

Experimental classification rule: below P16 = lower performance relative to the reference; P16–P84 = broad reference range; above P84 = higher performance relative to the reference. These correspond approximately to ±1 SD only for a normal distribution. Source-provided percentile cut-offs take priority where the distribution is skewed. “Mode” is retained when a source reports it, but many continuous cognitive datasets do not publish a useful modal value; median/IQR is often more appropriate for skewed timing or error data.

NUMERICAL COMPARISON ENGINE

How population averages and individual scores are compared

Mean + SD

When the participant completes the same or genuinely compatible task, the page calculates z = (score − mean) / SD. If lower scores are better, the direction is reversed. A normal-distribution percentile can then be estimated from z only when that assumption is supported. Mean and SD alone do not establish the shape of the distribution. Example conventions: z=0 is the mean; z≈+1 is about P84; z≈−1 is about P16.

Median + IQR

For skewed data such as reaction-time or error counts, median and IQR are retained. The page does not turn them into a z-score unless the original distribution or validated percentile table supports that step. A raw difference from the median is not automatically a percentile.

Mode & empirical percentile

The mode is the most frequent observed value; ties can yield multiple modes and all-unique data have no useful mode. It does not mean someone’s cognitive peaks. For matching participant-level scores, this page uses the midrank rule: 100 × (number below your score + half the number tied) / sample size. This describes the supplied sample, with ties made explicit.

Proportions

A reference such as “93.9% succeeded” describes prevalence. One person's pass/fail result cannot be converted into a meaningful percentile from that percentage alone.

Standard scores

Formal reports may already provide standard, scaled, T or percentile scores. Those can be placed directly on their published scale (for example 100±15 or 10±3) without reverse-engineering raw test items.

Progress from a previous formal assessment

Progress is strongest when the same standardized instrument is repeated under comparable conditions. A raw difference can be reported descriptively; where reliability and test–retest/practice-effect data are available, a Reliable Change Index (RCI) or standardized regression method can test whether change exceeds expected measurement error and practice effects. Scores from different instruments can be displayed side by side after standardization, but should not automatically be called “improvement”.

Private assessment baseline & follow-up

Keep each earlier professional assessment as its own dated baseline. Record the exact instrument, edition and index; use the original report’s norms. A new quiz is a separate experimental measure and does not replace a clinical score.

Values stay on this browser and device. Saving across visits is optional; on shared devices leave it off. The page does not submit scores or reports to a server. Browser storage is not encrypted and is accessible to scripts from this website.

RESEARCH CONTEXT · FUTURE EXTENSION

From task performance to brain-network research

Computational neuroscience offers a future route for studying how independently measured brain features relate to task performance. The present experiment collects an original untimed MCQ battery and a separate browser reaction-time task; it does not collect EEG, MRI or a connectome. Its chart represents observed task scores, with professional assessment values entered separately and privately.

Requirements before a neuroimaging extension can produce individual results

The following are proposed design requirements, not implemented capabilities:

  • Define one target measurement and research question; record acquisition modality, scanner or EEG equipment, protocol, preprocessing, atlas and feature units.
  • Select a licensed reference cohort covering the participant’s relevant age range and other justified covariates; document recruitment, sample size, missingness and scanner/site effects.
  • Acquire consented individual measurements with motion/artifact quality control. A quiz score cannot be used to fabricate a brain measurement.
  • Fit and validate the reference model using held-out data; evaluate calibration, prediction uncertainty, subgroup performance and transfer to new sites before reporting an individual centile or deviation.
  • For longitudinal analysis, preserve dated acquisitions and comparable protocols; distinguish expected age-related change, measurement error and acquisition changes from a supported individual change.
  • Use an appropriate secure research environment for imaging and identifiers. This public educational page is not a clinical imaging upload service.
Peripheral considerations: what the scores can and cannot predict

Cognitive performance can be relevant to training and job performance, but no single score determines employability, business success or the “right” way of life. A 2026 review of contemporary meta-analytic evidence reported modest correlations between general mental ability and overall job performance in newer datasets, substantially below older headline estimates. Entrepreneurship research likewise finds associations between some personality patterns and entrepreneurial status or outcomes, but individual effects are generally small and heterogeneous.

Employment outcomes also reflect opportunity, health, education, discrimination, labour-market conditions, social capital, accommodations, finance, sector choice and employer practice. UK official statistics are recorded separately from the cognitive table, with their data year, age range, outcome definition and denominator. Compare group rates using percentage-point gaps or ratios; do not pool different years, surveys or impairment definitions. “Non-disabled” does not mean “neurotypical”, and autism statistics do not describe all neurodivergent people. For example, the Buckland Review reported that around 3 in 10 working-age autistic people were in employment, compared with around 5 in 10 disabled people overall and 8 in 10 non-disabled people; that is an outcome statistic, not a cognitive-performance norm.

Accordingly, high scores may support hypotheses about particular work demands; lower scores may identify support needs, compensating strengths or environments that fit better. Neither establishes a person's worth, career ceiling or entitlement to education.

Observed interaction evidence: hypothesis layer only

Longitudinal conversations can provide ecological evidence about behaviours such as sustained research, error correction, conceptual association, planning, reframing and persistence. They are valuable because they occur across real tasks, but they are not standardized conditions. This layer will therefore be stored separately from numerical psychometric results and will never be used to manufacture a percentile.

Age, education, language and test conditions

Norms can change materially with age, education, language, device, sleep, fatigue, practice effects and testing environment. A valid comparison must use the correct reference group and record the conditions under which the task was completed. Adult-only norms will not be used to classify a younger participant, and older-adult cohort data will not be treated as a universal population average.
