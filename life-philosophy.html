Life Philosophy

I have approached life by identifying the gifts, talents and abilities entrusted to me, developing them, and exploring where they might bear fruit. My breadth has a purpose: to give what I have been given a fair opportunity to become useful, fruitful and sustainable.

Why I have approached opportunities broadly

My Christian faith has encouraged me to act without knowing in advance which door will open. I have built modelling profiles, developed music, studied Scripture to share with others, gathered professional experience, worked towards books and other streams, explored sporting opportunities, and used the influence and relationships available to me. My intellectual and educational journey is also a way of developing a gift.

The hope has been that one or more of these paths would become established: meaningful work, stable income, creative opportunities, service, or a lasting contribution. There has been real activity and output, but the stable livelihood and sustained return I have been seeking have not yet followed in the way I hoped. That gap deserves to be acknowledged.

SCRIPTURAL ROOTS · KJV

Sowing, stewardship and opportunity

Act when the outcome is uncertain

“Cast thy bread upon the waters”

Ecclesiastes 11:1, 6 also asks which effort will prosper: “either this or that”. I draw from this an encouragement to sow in more than one direction, rather than wait for certainty before beginning.

The ground matters

“Some fell upon stony places”

Matthew 13:3–23 describes seed meeting different conditions, including stones, thorns and good ground. Jesus explains this in relation to receiving the word of the kingdom. As a personal analogy, it also prompts me to consider the conditions in which effort is being invested, alongside the effort itself.

Put what is entrusted to you to use

“Well done, thou good and faithful servant”

Matthew 25:14–30 contrasts servants who put entrusted resources to work with one who hides his talent in the earth. The talents in the story are money; my personal application is stewardship of my abilities, time and opportunities. I want to use and develop them.

Hope that a gift can open a door

“A man's gift maketh room for him, and bringeth him before great men.”

Proverbs 18:16 has been a source of encouragement. The proverb speaks of a gift opening access; I connect that with my hope that what I can contribute will find a receptive setting. These passages guide my approach without fixing a timetable for income or recognition.

A record of effort, and a basis for peace

I catalogue these gifts and abilities because I want an honest record of what I have done with them. Where I have taken ten or twenty different approaches to an area, consistently over time, I want that sustained effort to be seen. A delayed or absent return does not, by itself, show that I lacked ability, effort or faith.

I want to be able to say: I identified what I had, developed it, approached opportunities, and gave it a serious chance. Where I have done enough and done my best, I can be at peace knowing that I did not let myself down. I can recognise what I have contributed without making the outcome a permanent accusation against myself.

I remain willing to learn, refine an approach and choose where to focus next. That willingness can coexist with recognising the limits of what one person can control. My responsibility to act does not mean every outcome is mine to determine.

Conscience before God, and peace within myself

Where I have offered some of my best effort and best work, consistently contributed, and repeatedly put myself forward, I do not believe I am to blame simply because an opportunity or outcome has not worked out. I have taken a broad, stochastic approach: pursuing different routes over time because I could not know in advance which would succeed. That uncertainty was part of the approach, and an unsuccessful outcome does not make the effort insincere or erase the work.

For me, this matters before God as well as within myself. My conviction is that, where I have faithfully put what I was given to use and done my best, I am not guilty of neglecting those gifts merely because the hoped-for return has not arrived. I do not equate an outcome beyond my control with personal or spiritual guilt.

I can honestly examine what I have done, learn where there is something to learn, and still have a clear conscience about the effort I made. I want peace in knowing that I did not let myself down: I contributed, I tried, and I gave the work a serious chance. I do not have to keep blaming myself for what did not work out.

Fair access, meaningful victory and truth

I believe a victory deserves to be called a fair victory only when others have not been unfairly sidelined, marginalised, blocked or excluded to secure it. A medal obtained by obstructing other people's opportunity to compete may record who was allowed to win, but it does not establish who would have excelled under fair conditions. Deliberately narrowing the field to obtain recognition undermines the integrity of that recognition.

Access therefore deserves much closer attention. Bottlenecks can increase the chances of success for those already admitted while preventing capable people from being considered at all. Where someone wins by blocking others, the result cannot honestly be presented as proof that the best person won. We need to examine the rules, entry routes, resources and decisions that shaped the contest, alongside the final award.

From an epistemic perspective, this is a problem of truth: a distorted selection process gives us an incomplete basis for judging ability, achievement or merit. Treating that result as conclusive evidence of superiority turns an advantage in access into a misleading claim about excellence. My principle is that recognition should be grounded in truthful evidence and fair opportunity; an outcome cannot make a false claim correct.

Questions about the environment

When sustained effort across several areas does not become opportunity or a stable livelihood, I also ask questions about the environment. Are there accessible routes into work? Are abilities being recognised fairly? Are resources, funding, equipment, networks and institutional support available? Is the setting receptive to what a person can offer?

These are questions to investigate through the actual circumstances. My philosophy makes room for personal responsibility, the conditions surrounding a person, and the dignity of knowing that sincere, sustained effort has value even before the hoped-for return arrives.

I want the gifts to bear fruit. I also want peace in knowing that I have put them to use.
