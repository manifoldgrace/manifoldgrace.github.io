Systems

Some questions become clearer only when the system is viewed as a whole. This page maps the institutions, rules, technologies, incentives and cultural assumptions that interact before an outcome reaches an individual.

SYSTEMS MAP

Why a whole-system page

My interest in systems is broader than discrimination alone. It includes the way government, law, technology, economics, education, health, media, faith, culture and administrative procedure overlap, and the way responsibility can become difficult to locate when each institution sees only its own part.

That perspective matters especially when a rule looks neutral in isolation but produces a different practical effect once combined with data systems, organisational incentives, resource constraints or historical inequalities.

Clarifying what is unclear

As a student, researcher and Black woman, I am interested in where systems become difficult to read from the outside: who made the decision, which rule governed it, what data travelled between organisations, what discretion was exercised and what route exists for correction.

Systemic discrimination is one important application of that method, but not the only one. The same mapping can be used to study AI governance, higher education, welfare, transport, public administration, markets or institutional failure without assuming the conclusion before the mechanism has been traced.

SYSTEMS ETHICS · VALUE ARCHITECTURE

What systems choose to value

Facts and values should be separated without pretending they are unrelated. A factual record can establish what happened, when, where, by whom and with what evidence. A value architecture determines which parts of that record a company, government, university, community, church or individual chooses to magnify, reward or preserve.

Different institutions legitimately value different things. Companies may emphasise sustainability, innovation, customers and economic performance; governments may emphasise public value, rights, legitimacy, security and stewardship of taxation; education may emphasise knowledge, learning, access and intellectual development; communities and faith organisations may emphasise service, dignity, care and belonging. A useful societal framework should therefore create a common language of value without forcing every domain into one ranking.

Pluralistic societal value architecture

A working model begins with a shared ethical floor — truthfulness, human dignity, fairness, accountability, non-harm, transparency and respect for rights — and then allows domain-specific values to remain visible. Economic, technical, social, institutional, cultural, epistemic, environmental and human-capability value can then be examined separately rather than collapsed into one opaque score.

Recognition systems matter because they are not passive mirrors of culture. What institutions record, reward, promote and celebrate can shape incentives, behaviour and eventually social structure. Visibility is not the same as contribution; senior accountability is not the same as execution; presentation is not the same as production.

Contribution provenance

A stronger record would preserve the pathway contributor or team → dated artefact or action → recipient → decision → implementation → outcome → beneficiary, with evidence attached to each link. This makes room for management, sponsorship, technical delivery, testing, maintenance, care, documentation and frontline learning without allowing one layer to absorb all the others.

AI could help preserve and test these chains by connecting records, identifying contradictions, surfacing missing links and distinguishing documented transmission from mere chronology.
