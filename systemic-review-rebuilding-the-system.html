Systemic Review and Rebuilding the System

An exploratory analytical framework for examining organisational structure, operating rules, procedural integrity, cross-system consequences and preventive redesign. The objective is to understand not only whether a procedure was followed, but whether the procedure, safeguards and remedies were appropriate to the people and circumstances affected.

Scope and evidence status: This is a general framework and research direction, not a determination that any named organisation breached a law or procedure. Application requires the rules operative at the relevant time, reliable records, jurisdiction-specific legal analysis and consideration of competing explanations. Personal case histories and identifying details are not published here. No system implementation or empirical validation is claimed.

Three complementary levels of review

1. Structural integrity

Are governance, authority, responsibilities, controls and service architecture designed appropriately?

2. Procedural integrity

Did a decision follow applicable rules, evidence standards, lawful discretion, accessibility duties and fair procedure?

3. Systemic and preventive integrity

Could emerging failure be detected, corrected and prevented before it causes avoidable harm across systems?

Compliance with an internal procedure does not by itself prove that the procedure is lawful, fair, accessible or effective. Equally, an undesirable outcome does not on its own establish a procedural irregularity.

Distinguishing failure modes

Execution failure: an applicable, valid and accessible process is not followed because of an operational breakdown, inadequate resources, communication failure or defective control. Design failure: the prescribed process is followed, yet the policy, decision criteria, technology or accessibility arrangement is itself defective or fails an applicable obligation. A case may involve both, neither, or insufficient evidence to distinguish them.

Method: reconstruct the system before judging the event

  1. Define the service, population, decision and period under review.
  2. Map organisational hierarchy, delegated authority and accountable oversight.
  3. Reconstruct the prescribed end-to-end process, including exceptions and adjustments.
  4. Identify policies, contractual terms, human-rights and equality duties, regulations and other legal obligations that actually apply.
  5. Build a dated evidence timeline: records, communications, decision reasons, system events, omitted evidence and uncertainty.
  6. Compare the observed pathway with the applicable rules and a defensible baseline; identify lawful discretion separately from departures or failures.
  7. Assess immediate effects, cumulative administrative burden and cross-system consequences.
  8. Trace potential root causes and test preventive changes, including unintended effects and privacy risks.
  9. Monitor measurable outcomes, accessible remedies and independent assurance over time.

Evidence-based diagnostic rubric

The following indicators support triage and investigation, not a legal ruling, validated score or ranking of institutions. They must be assigned separately for structural, procedural and preventive integrity. A missing record is not proof of failure.

Green — documented controlApplicable rule and authority identified, evidence supports execution, and sampled outcome/adjustment or remedy is verifiedRecord evidence and any remaining risks
Amber — uncertainty or partial performanceEvidence incomplete, controls inconsistent, delivery delayed, or safeguards not independently verifiedRequest missing records, examine alternatives and assess interim protections
Red — substantiated material concernReliable evidence indicates departure from applicable requirements, significant control failure or foreseeable harm requiring urgent examinationEscalate for qualified review, preservation of evidence and proportionate safeguards

Diagnostic record for each integrity level

Record the process stage; current and historically applicable rule; decision authority; source and timestamp; reported experience; institutional explanation; competing interpretation; risk or consequence; proposed safeguard; reviewer; and assessment date. Keep personally identifiable records in protected evidence storage, not on a public catalogue.

Safeguards against false conclusions

Do not average colours into a seemingly scientific overall score. Distinguish legal duties from voluntary best practice, the existence of a policy from its implementation, and harm from established causation. Allow non-applicable and unresolved findings. Where a relevant issue is disputed or potentially consequential, obtain independent subject-matter or legal review.

Evaluating proposed safeguards

Proposed controls should be evaluated for legal validity, accessibility, completion and correction rates, time to resolution, repeat-error frequency, disproportionate impacts, administrative burden, privacy, human oversight and cost. A process map or algorithm cannot independently decide that a rights violation occurred; human and legal review remain necessary for consequential conclusions.

Research status and boundaries

This page provides a reusable reference for future investigation and collaboration. It is exploratory research, not a commissioned audit, active engineering project, published academic finding or allegation against any individual or organisation. Site updates are dated, and future refinements should preserve earlier versions and distinguish proposals from validated outcomes.
