Evidence Verification & Criminal Justice

How claim-level provenance, historical record reconstruction, contradiction analysis and accountable AI evaluation might improve the accuracy and reviewability of evidence in criminal casework.

Independent conceptual research · 3 November 2026 · One application of a wider interdisciplinary framework · Not a deployed legal or forensic system

Purpose, scope and limits

This research application explores a practical question: can a legal reviewer understand precisely what supports a claim, what contradicts it, what was available at the relevant time, and what remains unknown? The goal is more accurate evaluation of evidence, not increased conviction or acquittal rates, advocacy for any particular legal practice, or automated determinations of credibility or guilt.

Criminal justice is a specialist application of a wider cross-sector evidence-verification methodology. Read the separate cross-sector framework for teaching, academic Recognition of Prior Learning (RPL/APL), healthcare, accounting, audit and other fields. The present page is about criminal case-evidence evaluation only; it does not describe or govern academic credit recognition. No inference is made that a particular firm has inadequate systems, has reviewed these proposals, or has adopted them.

01 · WHY THIS MATTERS

Evidence problems the architecture is intended to address

Fragmented provenance

An assertion appears in several reports, but each may repeat the same original source. Apparent agreement is not necessarily independent corroboration.

Conflicting chronologies

Different records may use event time, creation time, upload time or later amendment time. The distinctions must remain visible.

Institutional record gaps

Employment, educational and public-body records may sit with different custodians or be unavailable; absence of a record does not by itself disprove an event.

Unreviewed contrary material

Material that challenges an interpretation must not be discarded merely because it conflicts with the emerging narrative.

Historic rule changes

A current policy may differ from the version applicable to the relevant event. A valid comparison requires the correct historical version.

AI-generated inaccuracies

A plausible summary may conceal a hallucinated citation, changed qualification, conflated person or unsupported inference.

Legal boundary: Relevance, disclosure duties and access to third-party information depend on the facts and applicable law. The CPS advises that reasonable lines of enquiry must be justified and proportionate, including when they point away from a suspect. The research here supports that disciplined reasoning; it does not replace it.

02 · GRANULAR DESIGN

A claim-to-evidence register: what a reviewable record should contain

Each assertion receives a stable identifier. Each source remains separately identifiable, including where several documents repeat one underlying account. The relationship between a claim and a document is recorded rather than assumed.

03 · SYNTHETIC EXAMPLES

Where historical institutional records could matter

Scenario A — employment chronology

A fictional case raises a disputed claim about whether a person was at a workplace on a given afternoon. An employer's rota, payroll record, access-control entry and later HR letter appear to concern the same period. A structured review would identify which records are contemporaneous, which are retrospective, what time windows they actually establish, and whether any are derivative of one another. It would not infer presence or absence from a payroll record alone.

Scenario B — education or training record as contextual case material

In a fictional criminal case, a school or training record might be relevant to a narrowly defined question such as attendance on a particular date. A registry export, learning-platform log and retrospective email may use different dates or status definitions. The review would record each source's limited scope and flag uncertainty rather than silently produce a single narrative. This is not Recognition of Prior Learning: evaluating academic credit or prior competencies is a separate institutional assessment process, explained in the education-specific RPL section.

Scenario C — public-body decision history

A fictional case references a decision made under an institutional policy. The review would identify the policy version effective on the decision date, preserve the later-amended version separately, link correspondence to each stage and note any missing decision records. A newer public webpage would not be treated as proof of what the older rule said.

Scenario D — disclosed digital material

A legal team receives a bundle containing messages, images, transcripts and document summaries. A review register can show which source items were examined, how search terms were chosen, which apparent inconsistencies need human attention, and which material has yet to be reviewed. The work must respect lawful disclosure arrangements, confidentiality and a defensible audit trail.

All examples above are invented to illustrate information architecture. None describes a real investigation, witness, suspect or outcome.

04 · PROPOSED WORKFLOW

From authorised sources to a human-reviewed evidence pack

  1. Define a bounded question. Record the evidential issue, relevant dates, authorised users and why each category of information may be needed.
  2. Check legal authority and proportionality. Determine whether the material can lawfully be requested, accessed, used or retained, and whether a less intrusive source suffices.
  3. Register sources without overwriting originals. Maintain source identity, custodian, receipt path, integrity details, handling actions and known limitations.
  4. Extract assertions with citations. Keep exact excerpts or accurate paraphrases linked to specific record locations; preserve documents and contextual qualifications.
  5. Resolve temporal and entity ambiguity. Keep similarly named people distinct; separately track event, publication, effective and amendment dates.
  6. Compare relationships, not just strings. Distinguish direct conflict from different scope, transcription error, independent corroboration and mere repetition.
  7. Report missing records and contrary evidence. Propose justified, proportionate further enquiries rather than treating omissions as proof.
  8. Require human sign-off. Reviewers decide whether a proposed inference is supported and whether it is pertinent to the actual legal question.
  9. Produce an auditable export. Supply claim/source matrices, chronology, reviewer notes, correction log, unresolved issues and versioned report metadata.
  10. Propagate corrections. Mark superseded findings and notify authorised downstream reviewers without erasing original history.

06 · HOW TO TEST IT

A reproducible AI quality-assurance plan

Evaluation should begin with fictional or appropriately synthetic records and a human-annotated reference set. Tests must measure both where a system succeeds and how it can fail.

Evidence citation precision

For every material assertion, verify that the cited source exists, is accessible to the reviewer and supports the precise wording. Log unsupported and misattributed citations.

Coverage and omission

Compare the system's extracted claims and relevant contrary material against an independently reviewed reference set. Record omissions and ambiguous edge cases.

Chronology robustness

Test timezones, uncertain dates, document revisions, retrospective statements and two events with similar labels but different dates.

Entity resolution

Test people with shared names, changed names, overlapping organisations and duplicated document versions; record false merges.

Contradiction classification

Check whether a flagged difference is a genuine conflict, a change of scope, a later correction, a transcription error or simply insufficient information.

Privacy and human override

Test access restrictions, minimal disclosure, adverse or exculpatory evidence handling, and whether reviewer corrections remain visible in later exports.

Minimum pilot outputs: test-set description; annotation criteria; versioned prompts and models; metrics with denominators; false-positive and false-negative examples; reviewer agreement notes; documented failures and corrective actions. Do not publish identifiable case data or confidential prompts containing client information.

07 · ACCOUNTABILITY & LIMITS

Necessary safeguards for criminal justice settings

  • Evidence neutrality: Preserve material whether it supports, challenges or complicates a proposed account. Do not use an AI tool to manufacture confidence in either side's preferred outcome.
  • Necessity and proportionality: Make third-party and device-related enquiries only on a legitimate basis with appropriate legal authority, scope and privacy safeguards.
  • Originals and chain of handling: Do not modify original evidence. Keep inspection, transformation, hashing (where appropriate), redaction and export actions traceable.
  • Role-based confidentiality: Prevent uncontrolled upload of sensitive legal records to public AI services; require appropriate agreements, access controls and retention policies.
  • Professional responsibility: No automated credibility finding, disclosure decision, legal advice, forensic conclusion or judicial determination. Competent professionals must review and approve material outputs.
  • Limits of verification: An authentic source may contain a false assertion, a real contradiction may have an innocent explanation, and an absent record may reflect retention or access limitations.
  • Scientific transparency: Disclose version, test coverage, failure cases and whether any reported result comes from simulation, a laboratory pilot or operational use.

Status: This is a publicly shared research concept, not a validated forensic workflow, legal compliance certification, service offering or assertion of access to police, court or client records. Any real-world implementation needs independent technical, legal and professional assessment.
