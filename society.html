02 · CONSTITUTION

Parliament, government and courts are different

  • The Crown: formal constitutional roles, including within Parliament.
  • Parliament (legislature): House of Commons, House of Lords and the Crown; scrutinises government and passes legislation. UK Parliament
  • Government (executive): Prime Minister, Cabinet, ministers, departments and Civil Service; proposes policy and administers public services. GOV.UK explanation
  • Judiciary: independent judges, courts and tribunals adjudicate law; not just another branch of a government ministry. Judiciary; UK Supreme Court
  • Devolved institutions: Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland have legally defined legislative and executive responsibilities. Devolution

A useful distinction: Parliament holds ministers to account; ministers direct their departments; the courts perform constitutionally independent judicial functions. These powers are related, but they are not one chain of subordination.

03 · CENTRAL GOVERNMENT

From policy to delivery

Cabinet / Minister→Policy department→Agency or other public body→Service / decision→Review / accountability
Who holds which position?
  • Prime Minister → leads government, selects ministers
  • Cabinet → senior ministers and interdepartmental decisions
  • Secretary of State / other ministers → political accountability
  • Permanent secretaries and civil servants → administration and implementation
  • Agency executives, ALB boards and contractors → delivery within their respective legal arrangements

05 · ORGANISATION TYPES

How agencies, regulators and arms-length bodies differ

  1. Ministerial department — Minister-led policy department, responsible for defined central government functions. Example: Department for Education.
  2. Non-ministerial department — Government department without its own minister, usually with regulatory or delivery responsibilities. Example: HMRC.
  3. Executive agency — Administratively part of its sponsor department, often delivering public services. Example: DVLA.
  4. Executive NDPB — Separate public body carrying out functions under a framework of ministerial oversight. Example: Environment Agency.
  5. Advisory NDPB — Provides specialised advice to ministers rather than running departmental operations. Example: Social Security Advisory Committee.
  6. Tribunal / inspection body — Carries out specialist adjudication or monitoring, with independence that varies by law. Example: HM Courts & Tribunals Service.
  7. Regulator — A function, not one legal category; regulators have several statutory forms. Example: Ofgem.
  8. Public corporation — An organisation carrying out commercial or public functions under an identified public ownership/classification arrangement. Example: BBC.

Arm’s-length body (ALB) is a broad description of a public organisation operating at a degree of distance from ministerial management, not a single legal status. Ministerial sponsorship, executive independence, legal duties and appeal rights vary. Government public-bodies guide

09 · INSTITUTIONAL TRACING

Follow the decision, evidence and redress chain

When multiple institutions are involved, trace policy ownership, operational delivery, evidence movement, decision-making and review responsibility separately.

Education and student funding
Education policy→Funding delivery→Provider records→Decision→Applicable complaint / appeal

DfE · Student Loans Company · OfS. Different roles do not mean the same review body governs every dispute.

Welfare, assessment and contractors
Policy / entitlement rules→Service / contractor→Evidence→Decision-maker→Review / appeal

DWP. A contractor gathering evidence is not always the public authority making the decision.

Housing and local planning
National legislation→Local authority / provider→Decision→Statutory review / oversight

Council structures · Housing Ombudsman. Social landlords, private landlords, planning bodies and ombudsmen perform different functions.

Questions to ask when researching any institution
  1. Who has the legal responsibility and what is their mandate?
  2. Who sets policy and who actually decides cases?
  3. Which organisation collected, shared or processed the relevant data?
  4. What independent route exists to challenge errors?
  5. Which dated sources prove each connection?
