Designing Fairer Systems

Access, progression, economic security and accountability: an evidence-led framework for evaluating how public and private institutions distribute opportunity, reward, risk and recourse.

Research overview · Dated research log

The research problem

Entry into employment or education does not necessarily translate into career progression, financial independence or housing security. A system should be assessed through outcomes across a person's life, not just its stated commitments or admissions statistics. Social mobility and Civil Service studies document unequal occupational access and progression; they do not establish that any particular family is subject to a hidden intergenerational cap.

Central research question: Can institutions reduce avoidable barriers to advancement while retaining legitimate standards, fair competition, individual agency and transparent accountability?

Five tests of institutional fairness

1. Access

Can qualified people apply, enrol, obtain support, secure credit or access services without unnecessary administrative, geographic or socioeconomic barriers?

2. Progression

Can competent people advance through transparent pathways, with portable skills and documented recognition rather than reliance on unwritten codes?

3. Economic benefit

Do pay, security and wealth-building opportunities reflect work performed and changes in living costs, including housing?

4. Risk distribution

Who absorbs unemployment, unpaid training, policy failure, appeals costs, precarious contracts and service interruptions?

5. Accountability and remedy

Are decisions explainable, independently reviewable, correctable and subject to proportionate consequences?

Reform hypotheses to test

  1. Publish progression rules: advertise role requirements, internal vacancies, pay bands, training, appeals and selection criteria.
  2. Recognise established competence: support evidence-based recognition of prior learning and experience, with fair review and no presumption that credentials alone measure competence.
  3. Measure long-term outcomes: track advancement, underemployment, pay adequacy and economic security, not simply recruitment counts or programme completion.
  4. Reduce avoidable administrative burden: offer accessible processes, interoperable records and proportionate evidence requirements.
  5. Provide independent remedies: record decisions, reasons, correction histories, review deadlines and meaningful remedies where error is established.
  6. Test for unintended effects: evaluate who benefits, who is displaced and whether interventions improve opportunity without shifting disadvantage to another group.

These are proposals requiring feasibility studies, legal analysis, stakeholder engagement and outcome evaluation—not demonstrated effects.

Evaluation design

Compare baseline and post-intervention outcomes by time, locality and relevant socioeconomic measures. Distinguish access rates, time to qualification or hiring, progression after entry, real earnings, household security, review outcomes and appeal resolution. Use comparison groups and sensitivity analysis where feasible; audit errors and missing data; do not infer intention from correlations alone.

Intergenerational case studies: examine documented qualifications, work responsibilities, employment history, pay, estate or housing records, and institutional decisions while safeguarding living people's information. The four-corridor historical work remains a contextual research strand, not proof of hereditary institutional control.
